## 1. Section summary

Chapter 2 argues that the Achtiname (also "Akd Name", "Patent of Muhammad") kept at St. Catherine's Monastery, Sinai, is authentic. The author says it was concluded in the second year of the hijrah, written by 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, sealed with the Prophet's handprint, and dated 3 Muharram 2 AH, which he gives as July 7, 623. Most of his sources say Sultan Selim I took the original to Istanbul/Constantinople in 1517 and left certified copies. The author raises Greffin Affagart's report (pilgrimage 1533–34) that the original was still in the monastery's mosque, and suggests Sulayman the Magnificent may have taken it instead. He also suggests the original may survive in Topkapi or another palace. He lists Atiya's surviving Arabic and Turkish copies (six Arabic scrolls dated 1737/38–1800/01 or undated, codices of 1561 and 1683–84, and 43 Turkish translations). He then points to the long series of Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk and Ottoman decrees (975 to 1888) and fatwas from all four Sunni schools, plus the Fatimid school, as a millennium of Muslim recognition. Next he takes on the critics: Burckhardt (the document says it was written at Medina, was addressed to all Christians, and has 22 witnesses), Moritz and Skrobucha (date, style and content; no duties imposed; taxes fixed by the Christians themselves; possible forgery under al-Hakim), Mouton (anachronistic witnesses Abu Hurayrah and Abu al-Darda'; the hijri calendar not yet in existence; derivation from the 878 Najran letter forged by the Banu Makhlad), Ratliff (the convert-abbot 'Abd al-Masih) and Hobbs's unnamed "observers" (a monks' forgery c. 1010). His answers rest on the early use of the hijri calendar, scribal re-dating, and the later addition of dates and witnesses. He also attacks Moritz's character as a German imperial agent and Nazi collaborator. He discusses the eagle legend, Latin anti-Islamic legends of the 9th century, Napoleon's 1798 decree, and a 1403 treaty that mentions St. Catherine. A "Commentary on the Content" section goes through the covenant's 18 items (11 for the Sinai monks, 7 for Christians generally) and links them to modern political themes. The Conclusions call the covenant authentic in the absence of definitive proof of forgery.

**Supporting arguments (in the author's words where possible):**
1. The date (2 AH) "is consistent with the chronicles kept by the monks since the early days of Islam" (p. 65); "the Covenant's date is confirmed by Islamic sources as well as the chronicles from the Monastery" (p. 80).
2. Late copies prove nothing about the date of composition (Iliad, Tanakh, Codex Sinaiticus, Qur'an and hadith analogies) (p. 66); "Simply because a covenant was copied in 1630, 1561, 1538 or ... the 10th century CE does not mean it was created on that date" (p. 89).

4. Undated copies may be older than 1561, and one may be the first copy made from the original (p. 66).
5. Affagart's 1533–34 eyewitness report places the original at the monastery's mosque (p. 68).
6. A shorter version of the treaty with St. Catherine exists in Islamic sources (Ibn Sa'd, 6 AH); the Prophet renewed treaties, so there may have been more than one (pp. 69–70).
7. Monastery records of a 625 CE delegation to Medina; traditions of earlier visits (p. 70).
8. The monks have always upheld it; they had over a century and a half of European rule in which to "come clean" and did not (p. 70).

11. Accepted by both Sunnis and Shi'ites despite their mutual hostility (p. 72).
12. Even Kara Mustapha Pasha, who called it a forgery, conceded the immunity and kept the monastery exempt (p. 72).

24. There is no evidence for Mouton's Banu Makhlad forgery theory (p. 86).
25. The eagle story and Latin legends of c. 850 CE show the Sinai stories circulating by the mid-9th century (pp. 87–88).
26. The 1403 treaty "allusion" predates the 16th century (p. 88).
27. The covenant's injunctions match the laws of the early Caliphs (Fortescue) (p. 89).
28. The scribe's pious formula shows the copyist believed it authentic (p. 97).
29. The 1858 Canea copy, and Griffith's statement that it was confirmed up to Abdul Hamid II in 1904 (p. 98).
30. Conclusions list: Muhammad's travels in the Greater Levant; his confirmed contacts with Christians; the "remarkable stability" of the account; recognition by the monks and by Muslim scholars of all schools; the discovery of Selim I's firman; and comparison with the Prophet's other treaties (p. 98).

## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section (only what is in the text)

1. **CE equivalent of the Covenant's date.** The author gives 3 Muharram 2 AH as "July 7th, 623" (p. 83 [PDF 108]); Hobbs gives "623 CE" (p. 76 [PDF 101]). The Monastery statement says "the second year of the Hegira, corresponding to AD 626" (p. 96 [PDF 121]). The monks' delegation is "625 CE ... the second to third year of the hijrah" (p. 70 [PDF 95]).
2. **Date of Napoleon's decree.** Tomadakis (quoted): "8 December, 1798"; the author on the same page: "the decree issued by Napoleon on 19 December 1798". — p. 70 [PDF 95]
3. **Reign of al-Hakim.** Author: "(r. 996-1021 CE)"; Skrobucha's quotation: "(AD 996-1020)". — p. 79 [PDF 104]
4. **Reign of al-Mu'tamid.** "the nominal Caliph 878-885 CE" vs "(r. 877; 878-879 CE)", same page. — p. 86 [PDF 111]
5. **End of Ottoman confirmations.** "Ottomans (1517-1805 CE)" (p. 70); Ottoman decrees "from 1519 to 1818/19 CE" (p. 71); latest edict "issued by Ottoman Sunni scholars in 1888" (p. 71); Griffith: confirmed "to the time of Sultan Abdul Hamid II in 1904" (p. 98).
6. **Fatimid decree of 1134.** Skrobucha's "firman of 1134" and the author's "In 1134 CE ... al-Hafiz" (p. 79) are not in the list of Fatimid decree years (975, 1109, 1110, 1135, 1154, 1156) on p. 71.
7. **Is the display copy dated?** p. 68: Burckhardt's copy was "in all probability ... the one dated from 1800/01 CE"; the table (p. 65) shows a roll dated 1215 AH / 1800-01 CE. But p. 97 says the display copy "is not dated" and only "appears ... to date from 1800/01 CE".
8. **Where Burckhardt saw the copy.** The author says Burckhardt "visited the monastery in 1816" and examined "the copy ... on display" (p. 68). Burckhardt's own quoted words say the certified copy he saw "is kept in the Sinai convent at Cairo" (p. 81).
9. **Fate of the original.** p. 69: it may "remain stored in some forgotten archive, perhaps in the Palace of Topkapi"; the search "must start at the Topkapi Museum". p. 74: it "eventually went missing from the Treasury in Istanbul", possibly destroyed. p. 77: "The original copy ... was lost." p. 98: the author questions the note's word "lost": "Was it not simply taken by the Sultan as opposed to lost?"
10. **Which Sultan sealed the display copy.** p. 97: the note "confirms that the first official copy, provided by Selim I ... in 1517 CE, was sealed with his signature". p. 98: "Since the name of the Sultan is not stipulated, it is unclear if it refers to Selim I or the Sultan who reigned when this copy, dating from the 1800s was made."
11. **Handprint unanimity.** p. 75: the handprint is "attested to in all of the ancient sources", with Van Egmont & Heyman's fingerprint the "sole exception". But Skrobucha (p. 78) says "signed by him with two fingers of his hand". Digbassanis (p. 74) says the palm imprint appears in "Some of the later copies", while the Monastery statement (p. 96) says Selim's certified copies "each" depict the handprint.
12. **Writing material.** The note and the author's Arabic version say leather (*jild*) "from Ta'if" (p. 97). Affagart says "a piece of paper" (p. 68); M.L.M.D.C. (p. 75), Burckhardt (p. 81) and Griffith (p. 97) say "parchment"; Stanley says "the skin of a gazelle" (p. 76). The author's own durability argument on p. 67 discusses paper and parchment.
13. **Witnesses: genuine or later additions?** The author calls the document "witnessed by other Companions" (p. 77) and argues Abu Hurayrah and Abu al-Darda' could really have been witnesses (pp. 82–83). He also says witnesses "may have been added after the fact" (p. 83), that "In all probability ... both the dates, the name of the scribe, and those of the witnesses were added at a later time" (p. 85), and that the list was attached "no earlier than" 'Umar II (p. 86).
14. **Scribe's name: fact or later addition?** 'Ali as scribe is stated as fact (pp. 68, 70, 97: "it is accurate that 'Ali acted as the Prophet's scribe"), yet p. 85 says "the name of the scribe" was "In all probability" added later.
15. **Date: confirmed or added later?** The 2 AH date is "consistent with the chronicles" (p. 65) and "the Covenant's date is confirmed by Islamic sources as well as the chronicles" (p. 80). Yet "the dates ... were added at a later time" (p. 85) and "the year of composition may be a later addition" (p. 97).
16. **Dating the witness list.** p. 85: 'Ali was added to the "rightly-guided" Caliphs only "during the time of Imam Ahmad ibn al-Hanbal (780-855 CE) two centuries after his rule"; that is the basis for calling the order suspicious. p. 86 then concludes the list was attached "no earlier than the rule of 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz (r. 717 to 720 CE)", which is earlier than Ibn Hanbal's time.
17. **Stable vs conflicting accounts.** The Conclusions cite "the remarkable stability of the account of his contact with the Monastery" (p. 98). Earlier the author called Hobbs's "There are conflicting accounts" "a sound historical assessment" (p. 75) and spoke of "various conflicting versions" that "differ in details and dates" (p. 76).
18. **"All" versions include the eagle.** p. 87: "According to all of the various versions which narrate the pilgrimage of Muhammad to Mount Sinai, an auspicious eagle appeared". Among the versions quoted in this chapter, only Stanley's (p. 76) mentions the eagle; Affagart (p. 68), M.L.M.D.C. (p. 75), Hobbs (pp. 70, 75), Burckhardt (p. 81) and the Monastery statement (p. 96) do not.
19. **Islamic sources on the Sinai contact.** p. 69: "a copy of a treaty with the Monastery of Saint Catherine does indeed exist in Islamic sources" (Ibn Sa'd). p. 77: it is "found in partial form in classical Islamic historical literature". p. 80: the date is "confirmed by Islamic sources". p. 87: "The story of Muhammad at Mount Sinai does not exist in any of the surviving Arabic and Islamic sources." (Treaty and story may be different things, but the statements pull against each other.)
20. **Item numbering in the Commentary.** p. 91: "The fifth item promised by the Prophet was freedom of movement". p. 92: "Expanding upon the fifth item, the Prophet addresses the issue of taxation, for a second time, in item eight", and successors might read "item five" as granting tax-free status only to the monks.
21. **"Over a century and a half of European Christian domination."** The periods the author gives on the same page, French 1798–1801 and British 1882–1952, add up to about 73 years. — p. 70 [PDF 95]
22. **Recipients: specific monastery or all Christians?** p. 80: "the Prophet made a treaty with a specific monastery out of gratitude for their kindness. Nowhere else is this the case." p. 81: a forger "would have had the common sense to include direct references to themselves" (implying the text lacks them). p. 82: the principles "applied not only to the monks of Mount Sinai, but to Christians around the world". p. 93: the first eleven items "applied specifically to the monks of Mount Sinai".
23. **Age of the oldest copies.** pp. 66 and 68: the oldest copies at the monastery date from Sulayman's reign (16th c.). p. 89 lists covenants "copied in 1630, 1561, 1538 or at some point in the 10th century CE", and in the same paragraph says "the oldest extant copies date from the 9th, 16th, and 17th centuries" (10th vs 9th century in one paragraph; this refers to the covenants as a group).
24. **"Every authority has accepted 1517."** p. 68 says so (citing Ratliff), but p. 73 reports "some scholars still suggest that the Covenant ... dates to 1517 CE", i.e. composed then, and Atiya frames the 1517 removal as "The story is circulated" (p. 74).
25. **Anachronism inside a quoted source.** Hobbs's quotation (p. 75) says the mosque, built "around 1106", may have been "a device to ward off an attack by Ottoman troops"; everywhere else in the chapter the Ottomans arrive in 1517.
26. **Poll-tax amounts.** Item twelve fixes the maximum at "twelve drachms" (p. 94). The author's economic note that follows asks "What exactly did ten dirhams represent in the time of the Prophet?" (p. 94). Ten vs twelve is not reconciled.
