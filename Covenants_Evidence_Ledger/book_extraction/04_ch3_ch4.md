## 1. Section summary

### Chapter 3: Christians of Persia
1. Morrow introduces the "Covenant of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of Persia". It is a Persian-language covenant that "survives only in English translation" and is strikingly similar to the Sinai covenant; some scholars call it a Persian translation of that document (p. 99).
2. He gives three possibilities: a very free translation of the Arabic, Shi'ite tampering, or (in his words "far-fetched as it may seem") the Arabic Sinai covenant being a translation from a Persian original (p. 99).
3. His main source is Leon Arpee (1877–1947), who in 1946 reported that the document "was preserved in the archives of the Armenian Bishop of New Julfa", had been granted to the bishop in the 17th century by the Shah (identified as Abbas I), and was rendered "from the version of Johanianz (New Julfa, 1881)" (pp. 99, 102).
4. He sets the covenant against Shah Abbas's 1606 relocation of Armenians to New Julfa (Isfahan) and Safavid diplomacy with Europe. He considers how the Shah might have obtained a copy: from Armenians, Christian monks, envoys such as the Maronite Petrus de Monte (1516), Father Scaliger (1628–1629), or Twelver scholars from Jabal 'Amil (pp. 99–102).
5. He answers Seta B. Dadoyan, who calls Arpee's claim "highly dubious" and says no such document exists in the cited book or the New Julfa archives. He argues that Arpee had no motive or ability to forge it, that a referencing error is possible, and that the New Julfa library had been "robbed" (pp. 102–103).
6. He relays Arpee's report that Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (8th century) authenticated the text against the original "in his own hand". He calls this "probably the single most important endorsement any covenant of the Prophet can receive" and says it would make the Persian version the original (p. 103).
7. The commentary covers: Christians as believers; Muslims' duty to protect Christians even outside Muslim lands; a tax of four dirhams per adult male (no more than twelve for the wealthy); the ruler called "Imam" rather than "Sultan"; marriage "permanently or only for a time" (which he takes as mut'ah, with an extended defence of mut'ah); Christians' obligations; and 'Alid formulae (pp. 104–107).
8. He notes that the covenant is dated to the fourth year of the hijrah, a date he thinks a scribe probably added (p. 107).
9. Conclusion: authenticity "cannot be established" (Dadoyan), but in Morrow's view "neither can it be disproved". The document seems to have gone missing, and Dadoyan's remarks on the Tanzimat (1839, 1856) are presented as evidence of legal continuity (pp. 107–108).

### Chapter 4: Christians of Najran
1. The chapter opens with Archbishop Addai Scher's verdict (282/602) that the covenant is "certainly apocryphal" and was forged by Christians. The covenant came to light in Scher's Arabic/French *Histoire nestorienne inédite: Chronique de Seert*, published posthumously in *Patrologia Orientalis* five years after his death at the hands of the Young Turks (p. 109).
2. According to the Chronicle, the copy was made from a document found in 265 AH (878/879 CE) with Habib the Monk in Birmantha. Habib said it came from an original in the Library of Philosophy, written on yellowed cow-hide and bearing the Prophet's seal (p. 109).
3. Morrow rebuts Scher point by point. He then digresses at length on the Young Turks (1906 party, 1908 coup, 1915 Assyrian massacres, "1916" republic, 1922 abolition of the Caliphate), the Armenian massacres, and Western or German sponsorship of extremism (pp. 109–112).
4. He names the "strongest argument" against authenticity: the covenant's absence from surviving classical Islamic sources. Against it he sets the Prophet's many contacts with Najran: a delegation in Mecca before 621 CE, a delegation in 630 CE/8 AH, the mubahalah, a 9 AH treaty, and a 10 AH "Treaty of Najran" (witnesses named) quoted from Qureshi (pp. 112–114).
5. He disputes Hitti's claim that 'Umar expelled the Najran Christians, citing evidence of Christians there into the late 9th, 10th and 13th centuries (p. 114).
6. On the witness list (thirty-two Companions on p. 115, thirty on p. 138), he answers critics' objections about Abu Hurayrah, Mu'awiyah, Sa'd ibn Mu'adh and Ja'far ibn Abi Talib. Using Shaykh al-Mufid's account (quoted at length), he dates the covenant to "the last year of the Prophet's life", since Fatimah would have been too young earlier (pp. 115–118).
7. He rejects other critics: Claude Cahen ("pious fraud of Nestorian monks of the 9th century"), Vrej Nersessian, Brandie Ratliff, Louis Massignon and Barbara Roggema. He explains textual variants as different readings of unpointed early Arabic script, using the two versions of the Treaty of Maqnah (Ibn Sa'd and Baladhuri) as an example (pp. 119–121).
8. The commentary (pp. 121–138) covers several topics:
   - the exordium and the covenant proper;
   - the Prophet's literacy, argued at length;
   - the hostility of the Jews of Medina versus Christian loyalty, with digressions on anti-Semitism, Jewish converts, Donner's "Believers" movement, and the names and numbers of the Najran delegation;
   - taxes: four dirhams, a twelve-dirham maximum, clerics exempt, and Thevenot's Ottoman evidence;
   - exemption from military service, freedom of religion, consent in marriage, and Muslim help in repairing churches;
   - the ban on using Christians as spies;
   - Christians' obligations, including three days' hospitality.
9. Conclusion: the covenant is "a historical find of momentous importance". It was first published in 1919 and reprinted by Hamidullah (1956) and 'Amarah (2002), but remains little known (p. 138).

## 4. Factual claims (non-date)

### CHAPTER 3: Persia

**A. Framing and the covenant's textual status**
- C3-1. Only "a small number of scholars and educated readers" know of the covenants with St Catherine's and Najran; "far fewer" know those with the Armenian Christians, "the foremost of which is the Covenant of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of Persia". p. 99 [PDF 124] [O/F]
- C3-2. The Persia covenant is "strikingly similar" to the Sinai covenant, "so much so that some scholars assert it to be a Persian translation of the same". p. 99 [PDF 124] [A: unnamed "some scholars"]
- C3-3. "This Persian-language covenant ... survives only in English translation" and contains components not found in the Arabic covenant it is said to derive from. p. 99 [PDF 124] [F]
- C3-4. Three possibilities: (1) a very free translation of the Arabic; (2) tampering by Shi'ite scholars; (3) "as far-fetched as it may seem", the Arabic Sinai covenant is a translation from the Persian original. p. 99 [PDF 124] [O]
- C3-5. Morrow says that his "intimate knowledge" of the Sinai covenant allows him to "confirm that the Covenant of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of Persia is not a complete and accurate translation of the former document". p. 99 [PDF 124] [O/F]
- C3-6. The Persia covenant "was supposedly written in Persian". p. 102 [PDF 127] [A: implied tradition]

**B. Arpee's account**
- C3-7. Arpee: the covenant "reproduces a charter, purporting to be ... originally granted by Mohammed ... to the Monastery of St. Catherine, at Mount Sinai, and intended by the Prophet to be valid for Christians everywhere" (355). pp. 99 [PDF 124] [A: Arpee 355]
- C3-8. Arpee: "New Julfa ... was the seat of a Bishop recognized as the spiritual head of all the Armenians of Persia, India and Java, and holding a charter after the Prophet's reputed original defining the rights and obligations of a Christian community under Shiite law" (235). p. 99 [PDF 124] [A: Arpee 235]
- C3-9. "To all evidence, Arpee was operating on assumptions." p. 99 [PDF 124] [O]
- C3-10. Arpee claims to have taken the covenant from the *History of New Julfa at Isfahan*. p. 102 [PDF 127] [A: Arpee, as reported by Dadoyan]
- C3-11. "Having read Arpee's works, he comes across as a serious historian and his own works are often cited by specialists in the field." p. 103 [PDF 128] [O]
- C3-12. Arpee "did not have sufficient linguistic proficiency as to replicate a 17th-century Persian edict nor ... enough knowledge of Shi'ism to produce a convincing product", and "had nothing to gain". p. 103 [PDF 128] [O]
- C3-13. Possible error: "It is possible that Arpee, a typist, an editor, or a printer made a mistake in referencing the Covenant." p. 103 [PDF 128] [O]
- C3-14. "Many archives contain works in their collections that are not listed in their catalogue." p. 103 [PDF 128] [F/O]

- C4-60. Brandie Ratliff "relies on Scher (1867-1915 CE) (602, note 1) and Louis Massignon (1883-1962 CE) (250-257)" and "provides no new evidence of her own". p. 119 [PDF 144] [A/O]
- C4-61. Quote from "The Monastery of Saint Catherine at Mount Sinai and the Christian Communities of the Caliphate" (17, note 11): "(a)spects of this letter have also been questioned; it seems to have been produced by viziers of the 'Abbasid period, who were of Nestorian origin and wanted protection for their community". The text introduces it with "she", right after naming both Ratliff and Roggema, so whose article it is remains ambiguous. p. 119 [PDF 144] [A]
- C4-62. Massignon's "sympathy towards Islam was ambivalent (Irwin 224)"; "he once wrote that he studied the language of the Qur'an in order to demolish it (225)". p. 119 [PDF 144] [A: Irwin]
- C4-63. "The Christians in the Islamic world did not have a history of falsifying prophetic traditions or documents." "virtually all falsifications originated from the center of power". p. 119 [PDF 144] [O]
- C4-64. Roggema mentions the covenant "in passing as part of her study on the story of Bahira". She calls the Prophet's declaration "clearly fictitious" (116) and the pact "certainly penned by Christians" (117), but says it "should not be dismissed as an absolute forgery, because it includes a list of obligations for Christians known also from the Pact of 'Umar, and it also echoes some of the Prophet's demands and promises to the people of Najran as contained in Muslim sources" (117). p. 119 [PDF 144] [A]
- C4-65. Roggema (117), block quote: the Chronicle's covenant has obligations similar to the Pact of 'Umar (three days' hospitality; no shelter for enemies). Abu Yusuf Ya'qub and al-Baladhuri include a Prophet–Najran agreement with the same pledge of non-interference in monastic and church affairs. The option to pay a garment's value in money is "in all likelihood a response to the stipulation ... that the community of Najran must pay 2000 garments each year". p. 119 [PDF 144] [A]
- C4-66. Roggema notes that some Jews were accused of the same (118, note 68) and asks whether the "Christian authors of these edicts" wanted to prescribe a policy "which in reality did not exist in such clear-cut terms" (118). p. 120 [PDF 145] [A]
- C4-67. "one may not question original, authenticated documents: namely, the existing copies of similar pacts sealed by the Prophet himself." p. 120 [PDF 145] [O]
- C4-68. For Roggema the covenant is "a cut and paste job drawn from previous prophetic agreements and the Pact of 'Umar". Morrow: "would it not be simpler ... to attribute them to the same single author?" p. 120 [PDF 145] [A/O]

**F. Script and variants**
- C4-69. "the letters of the Prophet Muhammad were written in a very elementary form of Arabic script." The Sinai and Najran covenants "were copied from early Arabic script into the more expressive 'Uthmani script". p. 120 [PDF 145] [F]
- C4-70. The Treaty of Maqnah survives in two variants, in Ibn Sa'd and in Baladhuri. Gil: "Certain expressions were already unfamiliar in Ibn Sa'd's time, and he tried to explain them ... not quite accurately" (29–30, note 27). p. 120 [PDF 145] [A]
- C4-71. Baladhuri names the tribe "Habibah", Ibn Sa'd "Janbah"; both readings are possible because ha, jim and kha, and ba, ta, tha and nun, were indistinguishable without dots. p. 120 [PDF 145] [F]
- C4-72. Baladhuri "obtained a copy of the document from an Egyptian who had seen the original in Maqnah". It was "written on red parchment, the script of which was already faded" (Gil 29–30, note 27). pp. 120–121 [PDF 145–146] [A]
- C4-73. The originals were probably in poor condition when copied; any mistakes "were made in good faith". p. 121 [PDF 146] [O]
- C4-74. "proliferation of transmitters ... is considered a mode of authentication" in hadith studies (Azami 33–42). p. 121 [PDF 146] [A]

**G. Structure and exordium of the Najran covenant**
- C4-75. The Najran covenant "is the longest of all the accords concluded by the Messenger of Allah". "Unlike the other two", it begins with the basmallah, and "Unlike the covenants concluded with the monks of Mount Sinai and the Assyrian Christians" it has two sections: an exordium and the covenant itself. p. 121 [PDF 146] [F]
- C4-76. Exordium: "covenant of protection granted by Allah and His Messenger to the People of the Book, the Christians, who belong to the religion of Najran or any other Christian sect". pp. 121 [PDF 146] [T]
- C4-77. It was "written by Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah to all of humanity, as a guarantee of protection ...". The verb *kataba* troubles Muslims who believe the Prophet was illiterate. p. 121 [PDF 146] [T/F]
- C4-78. Morrow quotes his own "Pre and Early Islamic Period" (*A Cultural History of Reading*, 526):
  - *ummi* (Q 7:157–58) can mean "an inhabitant of Mecca, known as Umm al-Qura", "Gentile", or "unlettered".
  - "According to the Prophet's Family, Muhammad could read and write in seventy languages."
  - "Maxine [sic] Rodinson, W. Montgomery Watt, and Albert Guillaume" hold the Prophet was literate before Islam.
  - The quote lists sayings on knowledge, including "Seek knowledge, even in China".
  - Prisoners of war who taught two Muslims to read were freed.
  - pp. 121–122 [PDF 146–147] [A: Morrow's own work]
- C4-79. The Imams said: "By Allah, he could read and write in seventy languages" (Majlisi). *Hadith al-tariq*: "The Imam is blessed in his majesty with the knowledge of all languages spoken by all living things." Sunni traditions show the Prophet, Hasan and Husayn speaking Persian; Imams are cited speaking Hebrew. p. 122 [PDF 147] [T/A]
- C4-80. *qara'a* means "to read" while *sama'*, *alqa* and *sarada* mean "to recite". p. 122 [PDF 147] [F]
- C4-81. Pre-Islamic illiteracy would show he "could not have forged the Qur'an" (Saleh 33) and would explain "why he signed an early engagement with the monks of Mount Sinai with his palm-print". p. 122 [PDF 147] [A + F]
- C4-82. *Bihar al-anwar*: the Prophet was given knowledge of all languages when Gabriel first descended, and taught 'Ali the scripts of previous prophets (xviii, 266, 278–282). p. 122 [PDF 147] [A]
- C4-83. Exordium quotations:
  - "it is forbidden for any man, whether he is a governor or holder of authority, to revoke it or infringe upon it"
  - "the Believers must not charge them with other conditions than those which are included in this document"
  - a violator "will carry his crime on his head ... he will thus be an imposter in the eyes of Allah", compared with Q 55:41 and 96:15–16
  - "the religion of Allah has been imposed and the covenant which he has made has rendered protection obligatory"
  - Q 2:159 cited

  - p. 123 [PDF 148] [T]
- C4-84. The covenant "has not been concluded by equal parties"; "they were terms of surrender and submission". p. 123 [PDF 148] [O]
- C4-85. "the ancient religions" alludes to "the Sabians, to the Magians, and perhaps even to the polytheists". - C4-87. The Qur'an uses metaphors of hard and sick hearts (2:10; also 2:74; 3:7; 5:13; 5:52; 6:43; 8:49; 9:77; 9:125; 22:53; 24:50; 33:12; 33:60; 39:22; 47:20; 47:29; 74:31; 83:14). The Jewish people "slew the prophets in defiance of right" (3:112; 4:155). p. 124 [PDF 149] [Qur'an]

**H. The delegation: names and numbers**
- C4-101. Exordium: "The Sayyid 'Absiso, Ibn Hijrah, Ibrahim the monk, and 'Isa the Bishop, came to see me, accompanied by forty horsemen from Najran ..." p. 126 [PDF 151] [T]
- C4-102. "According to early Islamic sources, the Christians of Najran sent two delegations to Medina." After the mubahalah the Prophet "prepared a peace treaty" (Qureshi 85). Qureshi on the second, larger delegation: "[i]t consisted of sixty persons. Prominent among them were Abu al-Harith al-Ayham and 'Abd al-Masih. In addition to them, twenty-four famous chiefs were also included" (85). pp. 126–127 [PDF 151–152] [A]
- C4-103. "Qureshi, it is clear, has confused the names." Per Sunni and Shi'i sources, the leaders were 'Abd al-Masih, "the 'Aqib, deputy or leader"; the Sayyid, "their administrator, whose name was al-Ayham"; and the bishop and scholar Abu Haritha ibn 'Alqama (Ibn Ishaq 271; Mufid 116). p. 127 [PDF 152] [O + A]
- C4-104. Proposed identifications: Sayyid 'Absiso "may be al-Ayham, whose father is not identified in the Sirat Rasul Allah or Kitab al-irshad"; Ibn Hijrah "may have belonged to 'Abd al-Masih"; 'Isa "may have been the first name of Abu Haritha b. 'Alqamah who is only referred to by his kunyah". p. 127 [PDF 152] [O]
- C4-105. Numbers: Ibn Ishaq gives "sixty riders, fourteen of them from their nobles" (270), Mufid thirty (116), the covenant forty. "in Semitic languages, such numbers are simply used to symbolize a large amount. Hence, the account in the Chronicle of Seert agrees with early Islamic sources." p. 127 [PDF 152] [A + O]
- C4-106. "Among the Arabs, as among the Amerindians, the isolated individual could not exist"; family, clan, tribe, confederation. p. 127 [PDF 152] [O]
- C4-107. Exordium: the Najran Christians "did not break their covenant", "united to wage war against the Jews", and "came to an understanding with the People of the Vocation". p. 127 [PDF 152] [T]

**J. The covenant proper: terms**
- C4-126. Opening: the Prophet is "the Messenger of Allah to all of humanity, who was sent to preach and to warn". ## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section

1. **Year of Arpee's publication.** "At the time of publication, namely, 1946" (p. 99 [PDF 124]) versus "introducing a fabricated Covenant of the Prophet in 1948 would have been scholarly suicide" (p. 103 [PDF 128]). Arpee's dates are given as "(1877—1947 CE)" (p. 99), so a 1948 introduction would fall after his death. Also on p. 103: "Most scholars in the 1950s did not type", offered to explain a mistake in a work dated 1946.
2. **Date of the Battle of Khaybar.** Ch. 3 gives "the Conquest of Khaybar (629 CE)" (p. 105 [PDF 130]; the date is attributed to Sunni sources). Ch. 4 says Ja'far "returned to Medina in 626 CE at the very moment that Muhammad was returning from the Battle of Khaybar" (p. 116 [PDF 141]).
3. **Number of witnesses on the Najran covenant.** "thirty-two witnesses from the Companions" (p. 115 [PDF 140]); "from a couple to over thirty" (p. 118 [PDF 143]); "witnessed ... by thirty prominent Companions" (p. 138 [PDF 163]). p. 131 [PDF 156] says it was witnessed by "his closest Companions as well as the leaders of the Christians", whereas pp. 115 and 138 speak only of Companions.
4. **Scher's death and the first publication.** Scher died in 1915 (pp. 109–110, 112, 119) and the Chronicle was published "five years after his death" (p. 109 [PDF 134]), which implies about 1920. Yet the covenant "was first published in an obscure Orientalist journal in 1919" (p. 138 [PDF 163]).
5. **Ibn Ishaq and the Jewish–Christian dispute.** "The debate between the Jews and Christians, which took place in the presence of the Prophet, is not mentioned by Ibn Ishaq" (p. 118 [PDF 143]) versus "Ibn Ishaq mentions that a dispute between Christians and Jews took place in the presence of the Prophet (258)" (p. 130 [PDF 155]).
6. **Did Najran's Christians keep faith?** "When the Christians of Najran did not honor their accord with the Prophet, the Messenger of Allah sent them a stern, clear warning" (p. 113 [PDF 138]) versus "Unlike many others, this particular group of Christians from Najran had not betrayed the trust of the Prophet" and the quoted text "they did not break their covenant" (p. 127 [PDF 152]).
7. **Dating of the Najran covenant.**
   - Initially "the immediate aftermath of the Battle of the Trench or the Battle of Khaybar" (p. 115).
   - Then the Shi'ite "after the conquest (of Mecca)" chronology is called most sensible (p. 116).
   - Then "must necessarily date from the last year of the Prophet's life" (p. 118).
   - Morrow's defence of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh as witness rests on the covenant being concluded "after the Battle of the Trench", when Sa'd "may still have been in a position to ratify it" (p. 115). That sits uneasily with the last-year dating, given his reported death after the Trench (p. 115).
   - The delegation dates are 630 CE/8 AH (p. 112), with the jizyah treaty in 9 AH and the further delegation in 10 AH (p. 113).
8. **Number of Najran delegations.** "the Christians of Najran sent two delegations to Medina" (p. 126 [PDF 151], per early Islamic sources / Qureshi 85). But the chapter also describes a Mecca delegation before 621 (p. 112), a 630/8 AH delegation of sixty riders (p. 112) followed by the mubahalah (p. 113), the 9 AH treaty, and a 10 AH delegation (p. 113). Qureshi places the sixty-person delegation second, after the mubahalah delegation (p. 127). Morrow's p. 112 account appears to treat Ibn Ishaq's sixty riders as the mubahalah delegation.
9. **Size of the delegation.** "sixty riders, led by fourteen nobles" (p. 112, Ibn Ishaq); "thirty of the Christians" (Mufid, p. 117); "forty horsemen" (covenant, p. 126); "sixty persons ... twenty-four famous chiefs" (Qureshi, p. 127); "some twenty Christians" in Mecca (Ibn Ishaq, p. 112). Morrow acknowledges and reconciles these on p. 127 (numbers "symbolize a large amount").
10. **'Abd al-Masih and the 'Aqib.** Morrow: "'Abd al-Masih, who was the 'Aqib, deputy or leader" (p. 127 [PDF 152]). His own Mufid quotation lists them separately: "thirty of the Christians who included the deputy (al-'aqib), the chief (al-sayyid) and 'Abd al-Masih", and "The bishop looked at the deputy, the chief and 'Abd al-Masih" (p. 117 [PDF 142]). Morrow cites "Mufid 116" for his identification.
11. **Names of the Najran leader / recipient.** The exordium names "The Sayyid 'Absiso" (p. 126); the covenant proper is "concluded with Sayyid Ibn Harith ibn Ka'b" (p. 132); the requesters are "three people (from Sayyid Ghassani)" (p. 131); Qureshi has "Abu al-Harith al-Ayham" (p. 127); Morrow names the Sayyid "al-Ayham" (p. 127). Morrow reconciles only 'Absiso, Ibn Hijrah and 'Isa.
12. **Name spellings for the same person.** "Petrus de Monte" (p. 100) / "Petrus del Monte" (p. 101). "Girangos Vartabed" (p. 99) / "Giragos Vartabed" (p. 103). "Nercessian" in the citation versus "Nersessian" in the text (p. 119). "'Abassid" versus "'Abbasid" (p. 119). Some may be OCR.
13. **Mount Lebanon versus Mount Carmel.** The diffusion argument centres on Mount Lebanon (Petrus del Monte; Scaliger's Capuchins). Then: "Still, Mount Carmel stands out as a source of diffusion" (p. 101 [PDF 126]), with Mount Carmel not otherwise introduced in this section.
14. **Textual status of the Persia covenant.** "survives only in English translation" (p. 99) versus "the existing copy of the Covenant ... dates from the 17th century" (p. 103) versus "the covenant in question appears to have gone missing" (p. 107).
15. **Hitti versus Morrow on 'Umar.** Morrow rejects Hitti's claim that 'Umar expelled Najran's Christians to Syria (p. 114). On the same page he proposes that 'Umar "offered the Christians the option to pay the jizyah or relocate to Syria with monetary compensation", that "some Christians immigrated to Syria" and "the majority" went to Kufah, and that 'Umar "exiled only the hierarchs and nobles".
16. **Witness practice.** "with rare exceptions, they were not signed by witnesses" (p. 115) sits beside the 10 AH Treaty of Najran, quoted as witnessed by five named Companions (p. 114).
17. **Spy and envoy clauses: a contrast between covenants, not strictly a contradiction.** Ch. 3 says the Persia covenant "lifts this restriction from lay Christians", allowing a Christian envoy whose "service be accepted" (p. 106). Ch. 4's Najran covenant forbids employing "any Christian as a messenger, scout, guide or spy" (p. 137).
18. **Ambiguous attribution.** The quote "(a)spects of this letter have also been questioned ... viziers of the 'Abbasid period" (17, note 11) is introduced with "she" right after "Brandie Ratliff ... and ... Barbara Roggema" (p. 119). Morrow then discusses Ratliff, and turns to Roggema "in passing" at the end of p. 119.
19. **Date attached to Nafziger and Walton.** Printed "George F. Nafziger and (b. 1949 CE) Mark W. Walton" (p. 128), so it is unclear which co-author the birth date belongs to.
