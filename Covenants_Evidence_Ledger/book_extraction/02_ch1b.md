## 1. Section summary

This part finishes the author's reading of the Constitution of Medina. He sets it against Athenian democracy and the Roman Republic and calls the Medinan *ummah* a pluralistic "Free State" in which Jews, Christians and even pagans counted as one community, against Orientalist readings such as those of Peters and Donner. He rejects Donner's thesis that "Islam" as a distinct religion took shape under 'Abd al-Malik, while granting that relations with Christians hardened under that caliph (Dome of the Rock, Arabicization, the Standing Caliph coins). Under "The Judeo-Christian Response to the Advent of Islam" he recounts conversions: Salman the Persian, 'Abd Allah ibn Sallam, Addas, a party of Abyssinian Christians possibly from Najran, Qays 'Abd al-Rashid and Mukhayriq. He contrasts these with most Jews, who he says rejected Muhammad because he descended from Ishmael. The section on the Jewish opposition tells the "orthodox" Ibn Ishaq sequence for Banu Qaynuqa' (after Badr, 624), Banu Nadir (exiled 625) and Banu Qurayzah (627; men allegedly executed). He then sets out revisionist doubts (Donner, Scholler, al-Kalbi, Barakat Ahmad) and contextualizes the executions with Karen Armstrong. "Consolidating the Monotheistic Movement" lists about 90 raids and expeditions and argues that the Prophet used the threat of force rather than violence, unifying Arabia at the cost of about 1,000 casualties. "The Prophet's Domestic and Foreign Policy" quotes treaties and letters at length: Banu Damrah (2 AH), Ashja' (5 AH), Najran per the Chronicle of Seert, Saint Catherine (6 AH/628 CE), Yamamah, the Negus, Heraclius, Muqawqis, Chosroes, Bahrayn, Oman, Ghassan, Dumat al-Jandal/Ukaydir, Abd al-Qays (8 AH/630 CE), Maqnah (9 AH), Adhruh, Aylah, Himyar, Jurash and Najran/'Amr ibn Hazm (10 AH). His aim is to show a consistent policy: convert, or ally and pay tax, and no compulsion in religion. The section then follows how Christians received the early conquests (Sinai, Egypt, Spain), Muslim magnanimity (Saladin, Selim I's decrees for Saint Catherine's, Ottoman protection) and the Sinai Bedouins. The "Conclusions" cover the Umayyads, Imam 'Ali's instructions on Christian subjects, later Shi'i figures (Zayn al-'Abidin, al-Sadr, Khomeini) and Hizbullah. The section closes with a polemic that the West under-reports colonialism, and with Qur'an 3:113–115 as an epigraph on p. 64.

## 4. Factual claims (non-date)

### A. Comparative politics; slavery (pp. 32–33)
1. In Athenian democracy, "the majority of peasants, who represented more than 50% of the population," were excluded from citizenship; it was in effect "pro-slavery democracy." p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT/OP.
2. "Islam did not abolish slavery in a single stage," but "established a system that would gradually eradicate it"; in Islam slavery "is not a permanent state"; slaves can earn freedom "by work or conversion"; "all children born of slaves are free in Islam"; freeing slaves is pious and "in many cases … a required act of expiation for certain sins." p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT.
3. Roman consuls "presided over the Senate and the People's Assembly, which was composed of representatives of military units"; the Senate "represented the aristocracy"; plebeians "could not rule, elect rulers or make use of land," which "was reserved for the patricians or nobles." p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT.
4. The resemblance between Greek democracy and current Western "liberal" democracies "is no coincidence." p. 32 [PDF 57]. OP.

### B. Constitution of Medina and the early Medinan community (pp. 32–35)
5. The Prophet prepared the Constitution of Medina "Determined to bring an end to the bitter in-fighting between the Arab war-lords of the tribe of Khazraj and their Jewish rivals," and so "established the first Islamic State." p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT.
6. Under the Constitution, identity was no longer based on "family, tribe, kinship, or even religion"; citizens were "one and indivisible regardless of religion," whether heathen, People of the Book or Muslims. p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT/OP.
7. Clause quoted: "The Jews of Banu 'Awf are one community with the believers." p. 32 [PDF 57]. FACT (quoting the Constitution).
8. Peters quote: "If the Jews were permitted from the outset to practice their religion within the newly constituted ummah, then Muhammad's original Medina 'community' was a purely secular one…" p. 32 [PDF 57]. ATTR Peters 201.
9. The original Islamic State has been called "a kingdom, a theocracy, or even a secular state, all of which definitions are incorrect." p. 32 [PDF 57]. OP.
10. Armstrong quote: "In the Qur'anic vision there is no dichotomy between the sacred and the profane … The aim was tawhid (making one)…" p. 32 [PDF 57]. ATTR Armstrong 2000: 14–15.
11. In the Medinan community "all privileges of class were abolished"; rich and poor, Arabs and non-Arabs, black and white, men and women, children and adults "all had the same rights." p. 33 [PDF 58]. FACT/OP.
12. The Prophet said, as a rhetorical question, that even if his daughter Fatimah were to steal he would give her the punishment for theft. p. 33 [PDF 58]. FACT (hadith paraphrased; no source given).
13. "In the time of the Prophet, the ahl al-dhimmah [OCR "ahi aldhimmah"], both Jewish and Christian, belonged to the community of believers. The term kuffar was reserved for those who actively attacked and plotted to destroy Islam." p. 33 [PDF 58]. FACT/OP.
14. In "post-prophetic interpretations," People of the Book are considered *kuffar*, and only the best Muslims *mu'minin*. p. 33 [PDF 58]. FACT/OP.
15. Constitution of Medina: blood money replaces the *lex talionis*; it "devoted great detail to the peace process and fostering stable tribal relations." p. 34–35 [PDF 59–60]. FACT.
16. "The Arabs of Medina were known to be violent and bellicose warlords with a deeply-ingrained culture of revenge." p. 34 [PDF 59]. FACT/OP.
17. "Hypocrites" (*munafiqin*), "among both the Arabs and the Jews," tried to undermine the peace; defined as "those who outwardly professed Islam but secretly held different beliefs." p. 35 [PDF 60]. FACT.
18. "The Qur'an and the Sunnah were enshrined as the sole basis for legislation." p. 35 [PDF 60]. FACT.
19. Non-Muslims had the same rights, autonomy in religious matters, and "were not obliged to participate in the religious wars of the Muslims" ("rights without obligations"); Muslims and Jews were bound to defend each other, with "the cost of war … equally shared." p. 35 [PDF 60]. FACT.
20. Protection extended "to both combatants and non-combatants so long as they had not engaged in any crimes or atrocities." p. 35 [PDF 60]. FACT.
21. The Medinan state was "the first of its kind in the intellectual and political history of human civilization," founded "more than thirteen hundred years before the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)." p. 35 [PDF 60]. ATTR Khan 1.

## 6. The author's key arguments and opinions

1. The Constitution of Medina created a "tolerant, pluralistic government which protected religious freedom," more genuinely progressive than Athenian democracy or the Roman Republic; he asks why it "is ignored in favor of" them. pp. 32–33 [PDF 57–58].
2. The original Islamic State was "a unique system which had never existed before and which has never been seen since"; it was neither kingdom, theocracy nor secular state. p. 32 [PDF 57].
3. The Prophet's Community of Believers included Jews and Christians, yet Islam had a distinct identity from the start. He partly agrees and partly disagrees with Donner. p. 33 [PDF 58].
4. Donner misreads Arabic on *islam*/*al-Islam*; his 'Abd al-Malik thesis has "no basis," since the Prophet's own surviving letters name Islam and Muslims. pp. 33–34 [PDF 58–59].
5. Islam came "to correct" and "complete" Judaism and Christianity, which had "gone astray in matters of orthodoxy and orthopraxy." p. 33 [PDF 58].
6. The reverse of coin 89A "represents a broken cross" and marks "a monumental change in the relationship between Islam and Christianity." p. 34 [PDF 59].
7. The Prophet hoped the Jews of Yathrib would convert, because the learned Christian Salman had recognized him. p. 36 [PDF 61].
8. The Prophet showed mercy in exiling, rather than killing, the Qaynuqa' and Nadir, though killing them was "within his military rights." p. 39 [PDF 64].
9. The Qurayzah execution may be unhistorical ("increasingly, the evidence suggests that we cannot" believe it). If it happened, it "must not be judged according to the standards of our times." p. 41 [PDF 66].
10. Ibn Hisham, rather than Ibn Ishaq, was "More likely" responsible for the polemical view of the Jews. p. 41 [PDF 66].
11. The Prophet's harder treatment of the Jews compared with the Christians is explained by Jewish hostility and alliance with the polytheists (with Qureshi: Christians "were not active in Arabia"). p. 41 [PDF 66].
12. Both the Orientalist view (Islam spread by forced conversion) and the apologist view ("peace jihad") are wrong. The Prophet offered benefits, or else conditions, "a time honored political and military strategy." p. 42 [PDF 67].
13. The Prophet "did not use violence per se to bring people into his fold" but "use[d] the threat of violence as a stratagem." p. 43 [PDF 68].
14. Being a "man of peace" does not conflict with waging war, because social interests override individual ones and "the essential and irreplaceable ingredient for peace was justice." pp. 43–44 [PDF 68–69].
15. The Prophet was "a visionary long-term planner" who knew the spread of Islam "could take centuries." p. 44 [PDF 69].
16. Religious diversity is God-willed: no compulsion; the conquered pay taxes but are not forced to convert. p. 44 [PDF 69].
17. *Jizyah* "may seem startling to modern minds" but was "quite revolutionary." p. 48 [PDF 73].
18. By rejecting the Prophet and forcing baptism, Heraclius "sealed his fate" as Jews and persecuted Christians sided with the Muslims. p. 48 [PDF 73].
19. "Those who embrace Islam have nothing to fear and nothing to lose" (from the Bahrayn letters). p. 49 [PDF 74].
20. "Islam should be introduced in stages and taught by example" (from the instructions to Mu'adh). p. 56 [PDF 81].
21. Ukaydir treaty: Qureshi's translation is "poor." "False gods" may refer to Jesus as worshipped by Christians and "idols" to statues of Jesus, Mary and the saints. The Ghassanids may have been only partly Christianized or have ruled a mainly pagan population, so the Prophet was "helping purify Christianity from invasive pagan influences." pp. 52–53 [PDF 77–78].
22. The Prophet "was clearly no imperialist"; he aimed at a "United States of Islam" or "Islamic Union" in which regional rule was encouraged as long as solidarity with Islam came first. p. 58 [PDF 83].
23. Muslims must live in, or strive to set up, an Islamic State. Muslims preferring "monarchy, despotic dictatorship or 'democracy'" contradict the Qur'an and Sunnah, and a scholar opposing Islamic rule "calls his own orthodoxy into question." p. 58 [PDF 83].
24. Against Crone and Cook, the conquerors' indifference to converting monks shows they were "Qur'anically-conscious, bona fide, believing, Muslims." p. 60 [PDF 85].
25. Karabell's Monophysite-similarity explanation for Egypt's quick fall "is not completely convincing," since most Iberian Catholics also embraced Islam. p. 59 [PDF 84].

Muhammad tolerated Christian beliefs about Jesus' divinity and kept out of sectarian disputes so as to build a unified *ummah*, while being stricter with Muslims. This tolerance "is exactly one of the characteristics of the covenants" and is why Christians accepted them. pp. 59–60 [PDF 84–85].
27. Those who sow Christian–Muslim discord follow the "Machiavellian slogan of 'divide and you will rule.'" p. 59 [PDF 84].
28. Why Egyptian and nearby Christians sided with the Muslims "has not been satisfactorily answered." p. 60 [PDF 85].
29. "The historical literature is simply replete with evidence of Muslim military mercy." p. 61 [PDF 86].
30. The Sinai Bedouins' attacks cannot be blamed on Islam; they are the "desert Arabs" of Qur'an 49:14. pp. 61–62 [PDF 86–87].
31. Injustices against Christians under Muslim rule "rarely lasted long and were soon rectified." If the Umayyads, "the most wretched rulers of Islam," behaved well, the treatment by the Prophet and Imam Ali was "exalted." p. 62 [PDF 87].
32. Hizbullah's support for Christian candidates shows "an enormous amount of forbearance" given Christian atrocities in the Lebanese Civil War. p. 63 [PDF 88].
33. Muslims had the "fullest rights, protections, and liberties" under Islamic rule, not Western liberal democracy, and the "periods of greatest intolerance have coincided with Western imperialist occupation." p. 63 [PDF 88].

## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section

1. **Coin issue dates.** "These Standing Caliph coins, issued between 694/95 and 692/697 CE": the second date range (692/697) starts before the first (694/95). Possibly OCR or typesetting. p. 34 [PDF 59].
2. **Coin numbering.** "the image of the man who appears on the back of coin 89c and the front of 89c" names the same coin twice. The other coins cited are 89A and 89a; possibly 89b was meant. p. 34 [PDF 59].
3. **Heraclius timing.** The letter to Heraclius was "probably sent in 627 CE" (p. 47), and Heraclius "took the opposite attitude a couple of years later," illustrated with "In AD 632" (p. 48). On the book's own dates that gap is about five years. pp. 47–48 [PDF 72–73].
4. **Muqawqis's attitude.** On p. 48 Muqawqis is praised for "tact," keeps the letter in an ivory casket and sends gifts. On p. 49: "both the Egyptian, Roman, and Persian leaders expressed anger and hostility towards the Prophet." pp. 48–49 [PDF 73–74].
5. **Ja'far's relationship.** In the Prophet's letter he is "my cousin Ja'far" (p. 46); in the Negus's reply "your nephew and his companions" and "your nephew" (pp. 46–47). The inconsistency lies within the quoted documents. pp. 46–47 [PDF 71–72].
6. **Camel zakat rates.** Himyar letter: "for every five camels, a sheep; for every ten camels, two sheep" (p. 55). 'Amr ibn Hazm letter: "for every one camel, two sheep; and for every twenty camels, four sheep" (p. 57). The cattle and sheep rates match. pp. 55, 57 [PDF 80, 82].
7. **Esposito's middle initial.** "John L. Esposito (b. 1940 CE)" (p. 47) and "John L. Esposito" (p. 61), but "John F. Esposito (b. 1940 CE)" (p. 60). pp. 47, 60, 61 [PDF 72, 85, 86].
8. **Expulsion and execution of the Jews: author's narrative vs endorsed doubts.** The author narrates the exiles of Qaynuqa' and Nadir (p. 39), says the Prophet "forced them into exile" (p. 41) and writes "With his Jewish opponents sent into exile" (p. 42). He also reports, without fully endorsing ("not entirely convincing"), Ahmad's view that "no Jews were executed or expelled from Medina during Muhammad's life," and Scholler's that the Qaynuqa' and Nadir episodes may not have happened "as we know it." He says "increasingly, the evidence suggests" the Qurayzah killings cannot be believed (p. 41), though p. 40 narrates them as "alleged." pp. 39–42 [PDF 64–67].
9. **Khaybar: fighting or not.** "The Muslims marched on Khaybar, surrounded the city, and compelled the Jews to surrender. One of the captives of the battle…" (p. 40), versus "the Jews of Khaybar surrendered without any significant fighting" (p. 41). pp. 40–41 [PDF 65–66].
10. **Jewish converts.** "Besides 'Abd Allah ibn Sallam, the only other prominent Jew to embrace Islam was … Mukhayriq" (p. 38). Elsewhere: Ibn Sallam's household and aunt converted (p. 38); "Some members of the Banu Qurayzah embraced Islam" (p. 40); Rayhannah and Safiyyah converted (pp. 40–41); "he won a small segment of the Jewish community" (p. 41). The word "prominent" may reconcile these.
11. **Bahrayn ruler's title.** "Letter to the King of Bahrayn" (p. 49), "Mundhir ibn Sawa, the ruler of Bahrayn" and "the leader of Bahrayn" (p. 49), versus caption "Mundhir ibn Sawa, Governor of Bahrain" (p. 50). Spelling also varies: Bahrayn/Bahrain. pp. 49–50 [PDF 74–75].
12. **Abyssinian ruler's title.** "King of Abyssinia" (text, p. 46); captions "Emperor of Abyssinia" and "al-Najashi, Ruler of Abyssinia" (p. 46). p. 46 [PDF 71].
13. **Spelling variants for the same person.** Hawdah / Hawda ibn 'Ali (p. 45); Yuhanna ibn Ru'bah / Yuhannah b. Ru'bah / Yuhanna bin Ru'bah (pp. 54–55); Qureshi / "Qureishi" (p. 48); Rayhannah (single spelling); Muadh / Mu'adh ibn Jabal (p. 56); 'Amr ibn al-'As / Amr ibn al-As (p. 58).
14. **Gil cited as "Moshe."** Introduced as "Moshe Gil in his *History of Palestine*" (p. 53), then cited as "(qtd. Moshe 30)" and "(Moshe 28–30)" (p. 54), using his first name as surname. p. 54 [PDF 79].
15. **Duplicates in the forays list.** "the Third 'Ali" appears twice (p. 42 after "Jiddah," and p. 43 after "the Second 'Ali"), and "the Second 'Ali" follows the first "Third 'Ali." "Najd" appears in both the Prophet's own list and the forays list; "Awtas" in the Prophet's list and "the First Awtas, the Second Awtas" among the forays. "the Third Fadak" appears with no First or Second Fadak in the forays list. pp. 42–43 [PDF 67–68].
16. **Banu Damrah.** The 2 AH treaty is presented as a model of mutual aid (pp. 44–45), but on p. 58 the Banu Damrah are listed among those who "did not honor their treaties." This is not a contradiction in itself; it is noted for checkers. pp. 44, 58 [PDF 69, 83].
17. **Najran's denomination.** Goddard calls them Monophysite (16) and Betts says they turned Nestorian in 575 (5), p. 37. On p. 51 Rogerson lists Najran among Christian sites in a passage on the Monophysite Jacobite network of the Ghassanids. The author does not resolve this. pp. 37, 51 [PDF 62, 76].
18. **Joachim's lifespan.** "Joachim, the Patriarch of Alexandria (c. 1448-1567 CE)" implies a life of about 119 years (arithmetic on the printed dates only). p. 61 [PDF 86].
19. **Order of Najran events.** The Najran covenant via Sayyid Ghassani "apparently predates" the 6 AH Sinai treaty (p. 45), but "The year 10 AH also saw the complete submission of the Christians of Najran" (p. 57). These may be two stages, but the book does not connect them. pp. 45, 57 [PDF 70, 82].
20. **Ukaydir's religion.** The treaty text says Ukaydir "has embraced Islam, forsaking false gods and idols" (p. 52), while the narrative calls him "the Christian prince." The author raises this himself (p. 52–53). pp. 52–53 [PDF 77–78].
21. **Affagart's description.** He is "the French pilgrim" (p. 61) and "the French knight" (p. 62). Not contradictory; noted only.
22. **Unclear citation.** "(39)" after the Khaybar emissaries claim (p. 41) presumably means Scholler. "(12)" for the southern Jordan treaties (p. 53) follows a Qureshi 288 citation, but the source is not stated (Shoup 12 is cited on p. 51). pp. 41, 53 [PDF 66, 78].
