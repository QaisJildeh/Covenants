## 1. Section summary

The front matter frames the book as a recovery of "a third foundational source of Islam": documents the Prophet Muhammad is said to have issued to Christian communities. Charles Upton's Foreword and the blurbs make this claim. The book is also framed as a contemporary call to Muslim–Christian solidarity against "Wahhabi/Salafi" extremism and a secular "New World Order". "Concerning the Covenants" traces the European printing history of the *Testamentum et pactiones* / *al-'Ahd wa al-shurut* (the Covenant with the Christians of the World). It covers Sionita and Vitré in 1630, the further 1630 editions, Fabricius in 1638, Nissel in 1655, Nagy de Harsány in 1672 and Hinckelmann in 1690, then the translations by Rycaut, Briot, Grassi, Miltitz and Van Dyke. It also gives the earlier publication history of the Sinai, Najran, Assyrian, Persian and Armenian covenants. "The Covenants Initiative" prints a declaration and about 41 named Muslim signatories, and "Chasing History" adds news items from 2011 and 2013.

Chapter 1 reconstructs Muhammad's early life with a focus on his contacts with Christians:
- recognition stories from childhood (Jews testing him with a hen; Syrian and Abyssinian Christians);
- the Bahira episode at Busrah, defended against skeptical and polemical readings;
- the monk Nastur on Khadijah's caravan, and Waraqah ibn Nawfal.

The chapter's most distinctive argument is that the "twenty-five years of silence" in the sirah can be filled by the monks of Mount Sinai's tradition. On that tradition, Muhammad stayed at or worked for St Catherine's Monastery as a youth (shepherd, caravaneer, camel driver) and gave the monks a palm-printed promise of protection. That promise would be the origin of the Sinai *achtiname*. A long excursus covers:
- the history and dating of the mosque inside the monastery and the one on the summit of Jebel Musa;
- Muslim pilgrimage to Sinai and the reasons for its rise and decline (Fatimid interest; Crusades; Ottoman and Wahhabi/Salafi factors; closure in the 1980s; the 2013 closure);
- shared Muslim–Christian–Jewish devotion.

The chapter then summarizes the revelation and the Meccan period (610–622 CE). It prints a translation of the Constitution of Medina (from Ibn Ishaq 233) and argues that the Constitution created a pluralist ummah. In that ummah, Jews and Christians belonged to the "Community of Believers", and the author argues it was superior to Athenian democracy and the Roman Republic. He also rebuts Donner's thesis that "Islam" as a confessional identity emerged only under 'Abd al-Malik.

## 5. Covenant and document details in this section

### 5.1 Covenant of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of the World (*Testamentum et pactiones* / *al-'Ahd wa al-shurut*)
- **Claimed date of the document (AH/CE):** not given in this section. The contents give three versions: 1630 CE (Sionita's printed text), **1538 CE** (the "possible source covenant") and 1655 CE (Nissel). — contents [PDF 8–9]; xvii [PDF 18]
- **Original or source copy:** "what appears to be the original covenant brought from Palestine to Paris by Father Pacifique Scaliger in 1629. This possible source covenant, which dates to 1538, has never been previously published." — xvii–xviii [PDF 18]
- **Origin "especially obscure":** Sionita may have obtained it from a Levantine community or through church channels. Nissel's connection is "even less clear". The Ottomans, French allies, "had encouraged the dissemination" (author inference). — xvi [PDF 17]
- **Editions:** (1) 1630, Paris, Vitré, ed. Sionita, Arabic + Latin + Pact of 'Umar; 3 copies (Notre Dame, Chicago, Oklahoma). (2) 1630, Paris, no names; 1 copy, Harvard. (3) 1630, Paris, ed. François Hotman, Sionita's Latin, Vitré; copy at Marburg. (4) 1638, J. Fabricius, Latin only. (5) 1655, Leiden, Nissel, Latin + vocalized Arabic + Surahs 14–15; Columbia, VU Amsterdam and many European libraries. (6) 1672, Nagy de Harsány, Latin only, in *Colloquia familiar[ia] turco-latina*. (7) 1690, Hinckelmann, German. — xv–xvi [PDF 16–17]
- **Translations:** Rycaut (English, *History of the Ottoman Empire*) → Briot (French, 1670) → Grassi, *Charte Turque* (1826) → Miltitz, *Manuel des Consuls* (1838) → Van Dyke, *Capitulations of the Ottoman Empire* (1881), "not a direct translation". — xvii [PDF 18]

### 5.2 Covenant with the Monks of Mount Sinai (Achtiname)
- **Origin (monks' tradition as the author reconstructs it):** a promise by the young Muhammad before his prophecy, either oral and sealed with a palm-print, or a document written by the monks and sealed by him (Mouton 177). The formal charter dates to "the early days of Islam". — p. 15 [PDF 38]
- **Physical description:** "contract of protection, drawn up on the skin of a gazelle" signed with an inked hand-print ("(344)", p. 8 [PDF 31]); "written in Cufic characters upon the skin of a gazelle … marks of the Prophet's fingers" (Arundale 28, p. 12 [PDF 35]); a palm-print on "white paper" (Thévenot, p. 10 [PDF 33]); "an Arabic writing … Palm of his hand" for want of a seal (Cantemir 168, p. 11 [PDF 34]).
- **Signatories/witnesses:** "The original was signed by 'Ali, and Abu Bekr, and many of Mahomet's chieftains, the Prophet having consecrated it with his own seal" (Fazakerley 375–378). — p. 11 [PDF 34]
- **Scribe:** not named. The author links the Kufic script to "Imam 'Ali['s] personal artistic preference". — p. 12 [PDF 35]
- **Recipients:** the monks of Mount Sinai. Arundale calls it an edict "addressed to all Christians"; Thenaud says the privileges were granted "the monks of the Church of the East". — p. 12 [PDF 35]; p. 19 [PDF 42]
- **Terms (as travellers report them):** privileges and tax exemptions; Arabs who serve the monks subjected to them; the monks to feed the Arabs (Monconys); freedom from all tribute (Cantemir); perpetual privileges and "absolute power over the Arabs" (Morison); convent "forever sacred", land "as far as eye can scan" (Henniker).
- **Custody:**

## 6. The author's key arguments and opinions

1. Christian–Muslim conflict "has been artificially ignited by the forces of imperialism", and the covenants can inspire "insuperable harmony" among the three Abrahamic religions. — p. 1 [PDF 24]
2. Majlisi's account of two childhood trips to Syria should be believed "in this case". — p. 2 [PDF 25]
3. The Bahira event is historically plausible. A missing chain of transmission does not make it untrue, since the episode predates the hadith period. Skeptics (Bell, Armstrong, al-Dhahabi) are wrong or unsupported, and Gabriel "cannot, in good faith, ignore centuries … of sources". — p. 3–4 [PDF 26–27]
4. Secular scholars' "default setting" is denial, then distortion. The polemical labels for Bahira (Nestorian, Arian, Jacobite, renegade cardinal) are distortions. — p. 4 [PDF 27]
5. Bahira could not have written the Qur'an: it would have had to resurface 25–31 years later. The relationship "is not one of influence but one of recognition". — p. 4–5 [PDF 27–28]
6. Arian or Nazorean Christians, not Trinitarian ones, were more likely to be awaiting a final prophet; the monks Muhammad met may have been followers of Jesus who kept the Mosaic law. — p. 5 [PDF 28]
7. No one at St Catherine's could have taught Muhammad Arabic, judging from Antoninus Martyr's list of languages. — p. 5 [PDF 28]
8. Ibn Ishaq condensed his account, "perhaps suppressing elements that sounded too mythological". — p. 7 [PDF 30]
9. Muhammad was well travelled; the claim that he made only two trips is "a logical impossibility". The image of an illiterate Muhammad with little Christian contact stems from "an over-reaction" to Orientalist ideas. — p. 8 [PDF 31]
10. Muhammad probably combined religion and commerce in travelling to Sinai, may have been the monks' contracted caravaneer, and may have set off for Sinai for religious reasons or carried pilgrims. — p. 9–10, 15 [PDF 32–33, 38]
11. The Cantemir version "appears to be the oldest of all". It shows a cloud, not an eagle, and a hand-print used for lack of a seal, not because of illiteracy; the illiteracy charge "appears to be a later development". — p. 10–11 [PDF 33–34]
12. Selim I bought the covenant rather than seizing it, and would not have paid so much for a spurious document. — p. 11 [PDF 34]
13. **Central thesis of this part:** 25 years of Muhammad's life (ages 15–25 and 25–40) are "unaccounted for". When news of him "disappears in the Hijaz, news of him reappears in the Sinai", and the monks' account "fits" and "completes the picture". The monks were "experts in sirah literature, extremely lucky, or honest and truthful. The evidence points to the latter." — p. 12 [PDF 35]
14. The Qur'an "seems to support" a Sinai sojourn (93:6–8; 95:1–6; 52:1). The orphan sheltered (93:6) is read as the monks' care, and the "man created in the best of moulds" as the Prophet. — p. 13–15 [PDF 36–38]
15. "I would prefer a Muhammad who was mentored by monotheistic holy men than a Muhammad who cared for the idols in the Ka'bah." Islam is not weakened by continuing the Judeo-Christian tradition; "The issue … is not of borrowing, but rather of belonging". — p. 13, 15 [PDF 36, 38]
16. Muhammad's praying in caves may have begun in the cave of Moses on Sinai, "the immediate precursor to the cave of Hirah". — p. 16 [PDF 39]
17. By protecting the Virgin icon in the Ka'bah, the Prophet "re-enacted" the Sinai icon's Hand of God; "it was not truly the Hand of Muhammad, but rather, the Hand of Allah". — p. 13 [PDF 36]
18. The Bedouin belief that the mosque dates from the Prophet's time is untrue literally but "founded by his footstep", and expresses nostalgia for lost Muslim–Christian unity: "Something … had gone wrong". — p. 16–17 [PDF 39–40]
19. Placing churches inside Islamic complexes protected them, and the early Muslims "embodied the teachings of the covenants". — p. 17 [PDF 40]
20. Physical evidence (the minbar) "must always prevail over speculation"; the mosque dates to the early 12th century, while its "origin … appears to be lost in time". — p. 18 [PDF 41]
21. Thenaud shows the mosque was built or rebuilt by a convert prior. The Fatimids made Muslims the custodians of the covenant, and the earliest copies may have perished under Salah al-Din. — p. 19 [PDF 42]
22. Shi'ite (Fatimid) Islam had a "greater appreciation for sacred sites and spiritual pilgrimages", and Shi'ites alone still treat Sinai as a pilgrimage site. Salafis and "pseudo-Sunnis" do not, while "true, traditional Sunnis" do. — p. 19, 22, 25 [PDF 42, 45, 48]
23. Skrobucha applies "double standards" by treating Jewish and Christian traditions as fact and Arab ones as "naive". — p. 22 [PDF 45]
24. The decline of Muslim pilgrimage to Sinai is linked to the Crusades, trade shifts, suppression of inter-religious ties, the rise of Ibn 'Abd al-Wahhab, and Salafism, which "rendered the region unsafe". — p. 23–25 [PDF 46–48]
25. The Alawis of Syria and Alevis of Turkey may practise "a primitive form of Islam which has remained frozen in time". — p. 24 [PDF 47]
26. Muhammad was always a prophet, even before Creation. His early encounters with Christians confirmed his status and gave him confidence in "the spiritual solidarity of the People of the Book" (against Salahi). — p. 26 [PDF 49]
27. The Prophet visited holy sites and may have prostrated on soil brought back from Sinai. — p. 26 [PDF 49]
28. The Constitution of Medina created "a tolerant, pluralistic government which protected religious freedom". It was "a unique system which had never existed before and which has never been seen since"; calling it a kingdom, theocracy or secular state is "incorrect". — p. 32 [PDF 55]
29. The Constitution was more egalitarian than Athenian "slave-master democracy" or the Roman Republic, and the resemblance of Greek democracy to today's Western "liberal" democracies is "no coincidence". — p. 32–33 [PDF 55–56]
30. In the Prophet's time Jews and Christians were part of the "Community of Believers"; the post-prophetic labelling of the People of the Book as *kuffar* is a later development. — p. 33 [PDF 56]
31. Donner misunderstands "basic Arabic" (definite al-Islam vs indefinite islam), and his 'Abd al-Malik thesis has "no basis". Islam came "to correct … and complete" earlier religions. — p. 33 [PDF 56]
32. Donner's theories on Islam's origins "can and should be criticized as utterly unfounded". Crone has "little love towards Islam and Muslims". — p. 31 [PDF 54]
33. Front matter: Nissel's and Sionita's Latin translations are both "poor"; Haddad's and Naufal's reliance on a Turkish copy was "academically inexcusable". — xvi–xvii [PDF 17–18]
34. Front matter: Sionita's unusual orthography (no final hamza) "may have been a faithful transcription of the original text", reflecting the Prophet's Arabic. — xvi [PDF 17]
35. Front matter (Covenants Initiative): Muslims should publicly defend persecuted Christians "in the name of the Prophet Muhammad himself"; "Peace is … made by facing and enduring conflict". — xix [PDF 20]

## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section

1. **Date of Muhammad's marriage to Khadijah.** p. 6 [PDF 29]: Bahira "in 582 CE" and marriage "in 595 CE"; "In 595 … hired by Khadijah". p. 12 [PDF 35]: "from his voyage to Syria with his uncle Abu Talib in 582 CE to his marriage to Khadijah in 582 CE". Same event, two dates. p. 26 [PDF 49] ("wife … for fifteen years" by 610) agrees with 595.
2. **Start of the "unaccounted" period.** p. 6 [PDF 29]: "from the age of twelve to the age of twenty-five" he kept leading caravans. p. 12 [PDF 35]: "a period of ten years … from the time he was fifteen to the time he was twenty-five, that is unaccounted for".
3. **Muhammad's age at the Bahira meeting.** p. 2 [PDF 25]: "nine years old" in some Sunni accounts, "twelve" in others. p. 4 [PDF 27]: "an illiterate boy of nine or fifteen years". p. 6 [PDF 29] gives 582 CE, i.e. age 12 by the book's 570 birth date.
4. **Harb al-fijar participation.** p. 6 [PDF 29]: "spurious allegations that he participated … when he was twenty years of age". p. 12 [PDF 35]: Safdar Husayn "wrongly alleges … the Sacriligious War in 585 CE", which is age 15 on the book's chronology. The two passages also spell it differently: "Sacrilegious" vs "Sacriligious".
5. **Age at the Sinai visit.** p. 12 [PDF 35] places the Sinai stay within ages 15–25, before the marriage; p. 21 [PDF 44] speaks of a visit "at the age of twenty-five"; p. 8 [PDF 31] puts the Sinai contact in "this obscure decade" between the marriage and the revelation. That gives three different placements.
6. **Fatimid caliph name and reign dates.** p. 16 [PDF 39]: summit mosque built "during the reign of Abu 'Ali al-Mansur (r. 996-1021 CE), the tenth of the Fatimid Caliphs". p. 17 [PDF 40]: al-Hakim is "(r. 996-1021 CE)", and the monastery mosque was built under al-Hakim's "fourth successor, Abu Ali al-Mansur, al-Amir bi-Ahkam Allah, who ruled from 1102 to 1131 CE". The same name, "Abu 'Ali al-Mansur", gets two different reigns, 996–1021 and 1102–1131.
7. **Al-Amir's reign.** p. 17 [PDF 40]: "1102 to 1131 CE". p. 18 [PDF 41]: "al-Amir (r. 1101-1130 CE)".
8. **Certainty on the mosque's date, p. 18 [PDF 41].** "the origin of the mosque appears to be lost in time … the mystery of the Jami' mosque remains to be resolved". On the same page: "There can be little doubt that the mosque was built at the beginning of the 12th century". p. 17 [PDF 40] says "In reality, the existing structure was built" under al-Amir.
9. **Citation that looks like a date.** p. 16 [PDF 39]: the Jabaliyyah "believe it dates from the rule of Caliph 'Umar" is cited as "(Hobbs 634-644)", which looks like 'Umar's reign dates rather than Hobbs page numbers. Hobbs is otherwise cited at pp. 159–173 and 219.
10. **Number of covenants in the volume.** xvii [PDF 18]: "includes six of the covenants" but lists five (Sinai, Najran, World, Persia, Assyrian). Contents: six Part I chapters (with Armenian, Ch. 7) but only five text chapters in Part II. p. 1 [PDF 24]: covenants with Christians of "Mount Sinai, Najran, Assyria, Persia, Palestine, Egypt, Armenia, and the World" (eight).
11. **Route of the Night Journey.** p. 21 [PDF 44]: stops for prayer at "Medina, Mount Sinai, Bethlehem, and Jerusalem" (date "around the year 621 CE"). p. 27 [PDF 50]: "from Mecca to Syria … then to Palestine … and then to Mount Sinai" (date "620/621 CE").
12. **Closure of the monastery mosque.** p. 20 [PDF 43]: "officially closed in 1986" (Mouton; 'Abd al-Malik), and in the same paragraph "The SCA carried out a restoration process of the mosque in 1986" (Arabic News). p. 25 [PDF 48]: closure "in the mid-1980s" from a "chill in relations". p. 19 [PDF 42]: "In recent decades … the closing of the mosque by the monks and the planting of a cross on its dome".
13. **Waraqah's Gospel language.** The two Bukhari versions quoted on p. 27 [PDF 50] differ: "used to read the Gospel in Arabic" vs "would write from the Gospel in Hebrew". This is a variance between quoted sources, not the author's own claim.
14. **Fate of Sa'ib.** p. 8 [PDF 31]: Ibn 'Abbas says he swore fealty and received Hunayn booty, and Ibn Hisham says he "became an excellent Muslim". Ibn Ishaq says he was killed among the polytheists at Badr (338). The author notes this conflict between sources.
15. **How Selim obtained the Sinai covenant.** Sources disagree, and the author picks one: "demanded" in 1517 (Arundale, p. 12); "taken" (Monconys p. 9; Fazakerley p. 11); "purchased … for four thousand gold crowns" (Cantemir p. 11); "retained" after asking to look at it (Henniker p. 11). Author: "Rather than being seized, it was purchased" (p. 11 [PDF 34]).
16. **Which account is "oldest".** p. 10 [PDF 33] calls the Cantemir version "the oldest of all", although Cantemir (1673–1723) is later than Monconys (1646–47) and Thévenot (1655–63). The author ties its age to the "fabulous treatise by a Sinaitic monk" it is said to draw on. p. 11 [PDF 34] hedges: "If this is indeed the oldest account". The book also gives no date for Arundale's or Henniker's sources.
17. **Pococke vs Thévenot chronology, p. 5 [PDF 28].** Pococke (1704–1765) is said to have alleged the Sergius story, and "This fanciful fabrication was spread widely centuries before" Thévenot (1633–1667). This is not strictly contradictory (the fabrication may be older than both), but the passage reads oddly because Pococke postdates Thévenot.
18. **Arundale dates appear only once each.** p. 11 [PDF 34]: "Francis Arundale, the British architect, visited … in 1831" (no lifespan). p. 21 [PDF 44]: "Francis Arundale (1807-1853)" (no "CE", unlike the book's usual style).
19. **Spelling variants of key names.** Nissel / "Nisell" (xvi); Rycaut / Ricaut (xvii); Vitré / Vitray (xv); Dar Rahe Haqq (p. 1) / Dar Rah Haqq (p. 6); Bahira / Baheera (Salahi quote, p. 26); Jebel Musa / Jabal Musa; Morison's "Chanoine de Bar le Due" (OCR); "Hesburg" Library; minbar as "mimbar", "tnitibar" (OCR) and "minibars"; Omid Safi / "Saji" (signatory list, xix); "Mount Saint Catherine" (p. 14) for the monastery; "Propet" (xviii); "Zaffulla" Khan. The Göttingen library is listed twice (xv–xvi).
20. **Era phrase.** p. 25 [PDF 48]: Muslim Brotherhood influence "in the second decade of the second millennium". As printed; context (2013 events) suggests the 21st century was meant. No correction is made here; flagged only.
