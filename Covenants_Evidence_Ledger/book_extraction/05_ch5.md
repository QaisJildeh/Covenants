## 1. Section summary

Chapter 5 deals with the "Covenant of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of the World" (Arabic *al-'Ahd wa al-shurut allati sharataha Muhammad rasul Allah li ahl al-millah al-nasraniyyah*; Latin *Testamentum et pactiones*, *Testamentum Mahometi*, etc.). The author says it has no extant Islamic sources and an obscure origin, but was circulating in the Ottoman Empire and Europe by the early 17th century. He traces how it reached Europe. The Capuchin Pacifique Scaliger (René de l'Escale, Pacifique de Provins, 1588–1648) obtained it, probably in the Levant in 1627–1628. He brought it to France in 1629, and through François Hotman it went to the Maronite Gabriel Sionita, who published an Arabic–Latin edition in Paris in 1630 (printed by Antoine Vitray). The author disputes the story that it was found at a Mount Carmel monastery "one day's journey from Mecca" (Ricaut), and he rejects the allegation that Scaliger forged it. His key claim: he followed the chain of custody and found in the Bibliothèque nationale de France an Arabic Christian anthology, MS Arabe 214, copied in 1538 (1254 of the Martyrs / 945 AH) by Girgis al-Ifrangi, with the covenant on its final folios. He argues it bears Sionita's reading mark ("Gabriyal Sahyun al-Ziftawi") and so must be Sionita's source. Yet he also notes that its beginning, end, witness list (30 names) and scribe ('Ali rather than Mu'awiyah) differ from the 1630 print, and that it may instead be a covenant for the Christians of Egypt. He then surveys the reception of the text over four centuries, organised as defenders (Salmasius, Hinckelmann, Ricaut, Renaudot, Layard, Madrazo, Grassi, Twiss and others), neutral figures (Hottinger, Layard, Luke) and opponents (Grotius, Mosheim, Döllinger, Gieseler, Jacques and others). Along the way he covers the 1795 plans of the French Revolutionary Committee of Public Instruction to print it. He sets all this in the context of the Franco-Ottoman alliance and the history of Arabic printing. Finally he comments clause by clause on the 1630 text and then the 1538 text, drawing out possible Shi'ite and Sufi lexical features. He concludes that the covenant is a distinct treaty from the Najran covenant and that its variants point to authenticity. Whether the 1538 text is the forerunner of the 1630 text he leaves "to be determined".

## 4. Factual claims (non-date)

### A. Names and titles of the covenant
1. Arabic title: *al-'Ahd wa al-shurut allati sharataha Muhammad rasul Allah li ahl al-millah al-nasraniyyah*, "literally, the Treaty and Covenant which Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, Concluded with the Christian Community". p. 139 [PDF 164]. fact
2. The same title is rendered "The Covenant which Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, Concluded with His Christian Subjects" (p. 146 [PDF 171]) and "...with the Christian Community" (p. 149 [PDF 174]). fact
3. Latin names: *Testamentum et pactiones*, *Testamentum Muhammedis*, *Muhammedis Testamentum*, *Testamentum Mahometi*. English: "Treaty of Muhammad" or "Patent of Muhammad". p. 139 [PDF 164]. fact
4. The English titles are "identical to those applied to the Pactum Muhammedis", the covenant with the monks of Mount Sinai. The author therefore uses "Covenant ... with the Monks of Mount Sinai", "...with the Christians of Najran", and "...with the Christians of the World" (the last because it is addressed to all Christians). p. 139 [PDF 164]. fact/inf
5. The Turks called it the Prophet's "hand or signature" ("Hand of Muhammad", "Signature of Muhammad"). p. 141 [PDF 166]. attr: Scaliger 407
6. Layard: it "is usually called the 'Testamentum Mahometi'". p. 151 [PDF 176]. attr: Layard 207
7. BnF index title: "Charte accordée à tous les chrétiens, par Muhammad ibn 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib 12. F. 257-261 (foliotation)". p. 145 [PDF 170]. attr: BnF index
8. Other titles quoted: "Traité de Mahomet avec les Chrétiens" (Grassi); "Privilège accordé par Mahomet aux Chrétiens" (Miltitz); "Pact of Muhammad in Favor of the Christians" (Committee/Guillaume); "Original du traité de Mahomet, prophète des Arabes avec ceux qui font profession de la foi de Jésus" (Marana); "Testament de Mahomet" (Nagy de Harsany); "Pièce très rare et curieuse qui est le Testament de Mahomet" (Scaliger's chapter title). pp. 140, 152, 153, 155, 160. attr: various
9. Gieseler's titles: "Testamentum et pactiones initae inter Mohammedem et Christianae fidei cultores" and "Pactum Muhammedis, quod indulsit Monachis montis Sinai et Christianis in universum". p. 158 [PDF 183]. attr: Gieseler 563 n. 7
10. Salmasius wished "the translator had not given it that name; for it is nothing like a Testament. It is a league and a covenant". p. 152 [PDF 177]. attr: Salmasius, qtd. Bayle 1737: 38

### B. Origin, sources and discovery stories
11. The covenant "does not exist in any extant Islamic sources" (like the Sinai and Najran covenants). p. 139 [PDF 164]. fact

18. Ricaut: the treaty was found in a convent of the monks of Mount Carmel, "close to Mount Lebanon, and at one day's journey from Mecca, where Muslim pilgrims perform their Qurban, or sacrifice, prior to entering the City". p. 140 [PDF 165]. attr: Ricaut 320

25. Ricaut said "On dit" ("It is said") of the transfer to the King's Library and "quoiqu'il en soit" ("whatever the truth may be"), calling the document "ancient and curious". p. 140 [PDF 165]. attr: Ricaut 320
26. Scaliger's *Relation du voyage de Perse* (1631) does not mention a discovery at Mount Carmel. p. 140 [PDF 165]. fact

28. Scaliger describes it as made by Muhammad "in favor of the Christians before dying, and in the presence of the witnesses who have signed below, who were his disciples, & authenticated by the Secretary or Notary Public". p. 141 [PDF 166]. attr: Scaliger 407

32. Full French name: René de l'Escale dit Pacifique de Provins; born in France in 1588. p. 141 [PDF 166]. fact

### M. Content of the 1630 text (as quoted and translated by the author)
181. It does not open with the *basmalah*. p. 162 [PDF 187]. fact
182. Opening: "It was written by Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, the proclaimer and warner [*bashiran wa nadhiran*], to all the people [*al-nas kaffah*], ... [*wadi'at Allah*] ... a proof [*hujjah*] of Allah for the Christian religion [*din al-nasraniyyah*] in the eastern land and its west, its Arabs and non-Arabs [*fasihiha wa 'ajamiha*], near or far, known or unknown." p. 162 [PDF 187]. fact (quote)
183. Renderings of *wadi'at Allah*: Van Dyke "emanated from God"; Pococke "careful guardian of the whole world"; Arundale "confidant of God, and charged with the keeping of all created beings"; M. Mauchin's rendering in Baron Marie-Joseph de Géramb "the friend of God, and that he is charged with the care of all his creatures"; Skrobucha "promises of God"; Anton F. Haddad "as a trust on the part of God". p. 162 [PDF 187]. attr: these translators
184. Self-description: "This document [*kitaban*] ... constitutes an authoritative covenant ['*ahdan mar'iyyan*], a well-known certificate [*sijlan manshuran*], and a testament [*wasiyyah*]". p. 162 [PDF 187]. fact (quote)
185. Violation clause: whoever breaks it "has violated the covenant of Allah ['*ahd Allah*], denies the oath of Allah [*mithaq Allah*] and has disdained his protection [*dhimmah*] whether he is a Sultan or anyone else". pp. 162–163 [PDF 187–188]. fact (quote)
186. The covenant answered a request: "granted alliances ['*uhud*] and pledges [*mawathiq*] to those who requested it"; Christians placed under the safeguard of "His Prophets, His Chosen Ones [*asfiya'*], His Friends [*awliya'*]". p. 163 [PDF 188]. fact (quote)
187. Protection clause: the Prophet protects "their judges in my fortified borderlines [*fi thughuri*]", churches and places of pilgrimage [*siyahah*] everywhere, by land or sea, East or West, "the same way that I protect myself, my successors [*khatimi*], and the People of my Community [*ahl al-millati*]". p. 163 [PDF 188]. fact (quote)
188. *khatimi* "literally means 'rings' or 'seals'"; Qur'an 33:40 is cited. p. 163 [PDF 188]. fact
189. "ascribed authority [*nisbatu al-amr*]": the Arabic linguists the author consulted derived it from *nasaba/yansubu*, "to ascribe", and said it was "mostly used in Shi'ite texts". p. 164 [PDF 189]. attr: unnamed Arabic linguists
190. Economic clause: no harm from taxes and loans ['*ariyah*] or land tribute [*kharaj*], "except what they themselves consent to give". p. 164 [PDF 189]. fact (quote)
191. Bishops, Christians, monks, pilgrims and hermit monks may not be removed; churches may not be destroyed or their materials used for mosques or Muslim homes. p. 164 [PDF 189]. fact (quote)
192. "[i]t is not permitted to impose a capitation [*jizyah*] or any kind of tax on monks or bishops only that which they are prepared to give willingly". p. 164 [PDF 189]. fact (quote)
193. Wealthy Christians (owners of large businesses, divers, miners of precious stones, gold and silver) pay *jizyah* of at most "twelve dirhams per year", if they are residents and not travellers. pp. 164–165 [PDF 189–190]. fact (quote)
194. Travellers, and residents whose place is unknown, owe no *kharaj* or *jizyah* unless they inherited land over which the ruler [*sultan*] has a right. p. 165 [PDF 190]. fact (quote)
195. Labourers who cultivate the lands "are not to be taxed excessively". p. 165 [PDF 190]. fact (quote)

## 5. Covenant details by version

### 5a. The 1630 printed version (Sionita / Vitray, Paris)
- **Names:** *al-'Ahd wa al-shurut allati sharataha Muhammad rasul Allah li ahl al-millah al-nasraniyyah*; *Testamentum et pactiones*; *Testamentum Mahometi*; Treaty / Patent / Testament of Muhammad (pp. 139, 146, 149, 151).
- **Claimed date:** "the second day of the month of Rabi' Ashar of the fourth year after the hijrah" (p. 147). The CE equivalent is not given by the author; his sources give 626 (Hinckelmann via Basnage, p. 158) and 625 (Twiss, pp. 159–160).
- **Place issued:** not stated in the document as quoted. Ricaut (qtd. Bayle) says the treaty was made "in the monastery of the monks of Mount Carmel" (p. 153). The author says it was "supposedly" made with the Monastery of Mount Carmel (p. 164).
- **Scribe:** Mu'awiyah ibn Abi Sufyan (p. 147). The author's verdict is that the scribe and the date are "almost certainly Umayyad additions" (p. 167).
- **Witnesses:** thirty-six (p. 147); individual names are **not listed** in this chapter. Basnage's review of the 1690 Hamburg edition: "signed by thirty-three Muslims" (p. 158). Marana's spy: "four main legal authorities, Abu Bakr, 'Uthman, and 'Ali" (p. 160). Author: the list "with a few exceptions, seems reasonably sound, but ... was probably appended to it to appease hadith scholars" (p. 167).
- **Recipients:** all Christians, "in the eastern land and its west, its Arabs and non-Arabs, near or far, known or unknown" (p. 162).
- **Opening/closing:** no *basmalah*, which the author takes as Sionita's editorial suppression (p. 162). The text closes with "Allah suffices as a Witness" and praises from al-Fatihah (p. 167).
- **Source manuscript:** brought by Scaliger in 1629; passed to the King, Hotman and Sionita; "eventually" in the King's library (pp. 142–143). The author identifies it with BnF Arabe 214 (p. 145), but hedges later (pp. 168, 176).
- **Publication:** Paris 1630, Arabic and Latin. Credited as "published by Gabriel Sionita" (Fortescue, Layard), "Published ... by Antoine Vitray" (p. 146), or "edited ... by Antoine Vitray by Louis XIII with the collaboration of Gabriel Sionite" (Balagna). Printed without full vocalisation. Four reprints in the first year. Later editions in 1638, 1655, 1668, 1690 (Hamburg) and 1888. French translation by Scaliger in 1631; Nissel's vocalised edition; translations into Latin, French and German; French translation in Ricaut (1670); Nagy de Harsany's French translation; English translation of 1688; Lefebvre-Villebrune's French translation (1795, never printed); Grassi 1826; Miltitz 1838 (French from the English in Ricaut); Van Dyke 1881 (English from the French in Ricaut). Other renderings cited: Pococke, Arundale, Mauchin/Géramb, Skrobucha, Haddad.
- **Copy consulted by the author:** a copy of Sionita's edition with invocations handwritten by Valerianus Oserole in 1659 (p. 151).
- **Text quality:** the author finds its Arabic inferior to the 1538 text, with words "modified, thus completely changing the meaning" (pp. 146, 168).
- **Lexical notes (author):** *ahli* ("families"), *khatimi* ("seals" → "successors"), *nisbatu al-amr*, which may indicate passage through "Levantine Shi'ites" (pp. 163–164).

### 5c. Related covenants mentioned
- **Najran covenant:** in the Chronicle of Seert (Nestorian, 9th century); "discovered in a monastery in Iraq in 878 CE"; undated; scribe Mu'awiyah; 30 witnesses; written on leather (pp. 146–147, 168, 174).

- **Covenant of 'Ali with the Armenian Nation:** earliest copies from the 18th century (p. 169).
- **Treaty of 'Umar (Capitulations of 'Umar):** 636 (Twiss); alludes to an earlier covenant (Féraud-Giraud) (pp. 152, 160).

### 5d. The author's authenticity verdict and arguments
**Verdict:** a "distinct treaty" from the Najran covenant, "apparently produced during the same period"; its variants "are evidence of authenticity as opposed to forgery" (p. 175). The scribe and the date are later (Umayyad) additions (p. 167). The witness lists were "attached posthumously" (p. 174). He leaves open whether the 1538 text is the forerunner of the 1630 text (p. 176).

**Arguments for authenticity / against forgery by Scaliger:**
1. Scaliger lacked the Arabic and Islamic learning to forge it (pp. 142–143).
2. Scaliger was saintly and pious, with no motive, and could not fool the King's scholars (p. 143).
3. The covenant could not help convert Muslims and seems "better suited to attract Christians to Islam" (p. 143).
4. Christians were well treated under the Ottomans, so there was no need for a protective forgery (p. 143).
5. The 1538 manuscript predates Scaliger by nearly a century, so he "absolutely and categorically did not forge" it (p. 145).
6. Its previous owners and readers treated it as genuine (p. 145).
7. Bar Hebraeus, Maris and Amrus attest its substance, so Scaliger "could not have invented something that already existed" (p. 152).
8. Sultan Ibrahim I referred to a treaty "as old as historical memory" (p. 141).
9. The Muslims accepted it (Mosheim, Layard, Luke) (pp. 151–152).
10. The style differs from the Qur'an because the Prophet used different genres; the Qur'an is not "the product of Muhammad's mind and pen" (pp. 157–158).
11. Hinckelmann, an Arabic expert, reported that scholars' doubts subsided (pp. 158–159).
12. The 4 AH timing fits the conflict with the Jews and the need for Christian buffers against Byzantium (p. 159).
13. Twiss: the term "conditions" fits the Qur'an and the Treaty of 'Umar; forgers would not have produced terms contrary to Muslim law (pp. 159–160).
14. Forgers under the Fatimids would not have named Companions viewed unfavourably, nor asked for tax exemptions (p. 160).
15. A text that is not addressed to a specific community and asks for little is unlike a forgery; such forgeries "would have been detected, denounced, and duly punished" (pp. 175–176).
16. Similar but non-identical texts point to a common origin (p. 175).
17. Late copies do not imply forgery (the Bukhari and Qur'an analogies) (p. 174).

**Counter-arguments he addresses:**
- Ricaut's location story → rejected as geographically impossible (p. 140).
- Bayle 1735: Scaliger forged it → rejected (pp. 142–143).
- Grotius and others: fabricated by monks of Syria and Arabia → the author calls them prejudiced (pp. 156–157).
- Mosheim: Muslim acceptance is worthless because they are "illiterate" → called "racist and prejudiced" (pp. 156–157).
- The style argument (Addison's informant; the opposers in Mosheim) → "amateurish" (pp. 157–158).
- Brought by Scaliger, therefore forged → "does not constitute proof of forgery" (p. 158).
- Döllinger and Tychsen: it would put Christians above Muslims → "self-defeating", since Döllinger accepts the Najran covenant (p. 157).
- Jacques and Gieseler: spurious → attributed to hostility toward Islam (p. 158).
- Addison and Van Dyke: a temporary tactic later reversed → "evidently incorrect"; *kuffar* refers to polytheists (p. 154).
- Layard and Luke's picture of opinion → "slanted" and "factually incorrect" (p. 156).
- Guillaume: "apocryphal" → read as a shift in attitudes after the Revolution (p. 155).
- Mu'awiyah as scribe in 4 AH is anachronistic (he converted c. 8 AH) → explained as a later alteration (pp. 147, 167, 174).
- A copy dated 945 AH is a millennium late → it is "simply a copy" (p. 174).

47. Opposing the accord "would be an act of apostasy"; Muslims represent the Prophet by keeping it. p. 164 [PDF 189]
48. Believers should stand together "in opposition to atheism", and against "the secular New World Order". pp. 165–166 [PDF 190–191]
49. Religions are "ecosystems"; there is no place for syncretism; certain mixed marriages cause conflict. p. 166 [PDF 191]
50. The Umayyad-to-Ottoman ban on church repair "was un-Islamic and in clear contradiction of the covenants of the Prophet". p. 166 [PDF 191]
51. The war-service clause "prohibits coercion and allows for exceptions in case of voluntary service" (Mu'awiyah's navy). p. 167 [PDF 192]
52. "Allah suffices as a Witness" implies that "the document is authentic regardless of the chain of authorities". p. 167 [PDF 192]
53. Sionita's edition is "inferior to the source covenant". Either "Sionita's Arabic was not up to par" or his "corrections" backfired. p. 168 [PDF 193]
54. The 1538 opening formula and the term *al-asad* reflect Sufi and Shi'ite influence. The 1538 formula "was probably absent from the original". p. 169 [PDF 194]
55. Breaking covenants is "a mortal sin which merits eternal damnation" (Qur'an citations). p. 169 [PDF 194]
56. The 1538 lexicon is "richer", "more precise", "of greater lucidity"; *khassati* makes more sense than *khatimi*. p. 170 [PDF 195]
57. The wool-wearers clause "almost appears like an allusion to the Sufis", as if foreshadowing Umayyad decline; the Umayyads' "heirs ... are the present-day Gulf Arabs". p. 171 [PDF 196]
58. "twelve silver dirhams" is a "sign of updating of information". p. 171 [PDF 196]
59. The 1538 witness list is "more difficult to digest". Some witnesses may have been Jews or Christians in delegations to Medina, and the signatories were "attached posthumously". pp. 173–174 [PDF 198–199]
60. 'Ali as scribe "agrees with the historical record", whereas Mu'awiyah is "as unlikely as it is unpalatable". Sionita may have changed the scribe's name "to appease the Ottomans". p. 174 [PDF 199]
61. The leather writing surface suggests authorship "during the early Islamic period, prior to the availability of parchment". p. 174 [PDF 199]
62. The owner Sim'an al-Barallusi "was almost certainly a Christian". p. 175 [PDF 200]
63. The 1592 missionary may have carried the book "from Egypt to Mount Carmel". p. 175 [PDF 200]
64. A European long resident in the Middle East could have learned to copy Arabic, so Girgis "al-Ifrangi" could be European. p. 175 [PDF 200]
65. The final note shows "a Christian cleric, a mysterious Michel", urging that the Covenant be recited in congregational prayer, "mass and liturgy". p. 175 [PDF 200]
66. The 1538 copy "was clearly not crafted during a period of persecution". p. 175 [PDF 200]
67. Conclusion: the 1538 text may have been directed to the Coptic Christians of Egypt and the 1630 text to "the Maronites from Palestine"; "It would be premature to come to any definitive conclusions." p. 176 [PDF 201]

## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section

1. **Certainty vs. hedging on the 1538 / 1630 identity.** p. 145 [PDF 170]: "there is no question that this is the actual document that he [Scaliger] brought back". p. 168 [PDF 193]: "This must, of course, be the source covenant", yet "Or are we dealing with a totally different covenant?" and it "may represent a Covenant ... with the Christians of Egypt". p. 176 [PDF 201]: whether 1538 is the forerunner of 1630 "remains to be determined".
2. **Was it discovered or given?** p. 141 [PDF 166]: Scaliger's words suggest "the Covenant was given to him", and "no discovery at all". p. 142 [PDF 167]: "I remain convinced that ... Scaliger ... did indeed discover the covenant in a monastery in the Middle East". p. 142 also says "Whether he received it from Carmelite or Capuchin monks or from the Sultan himself matters little."
3. **Sent vs. delivered in person.** p. 142 [PDF 167]: since Scaliger travelled to Paris himself, "there would have been no need for him to send the covenant by courier". p. 152 [PDF 177]: "He sent this covenant to the King of France in good faith". p. 141–142: Scaliger "provided the document to the King" in person.
4. **Who first examined the manuscript.** p. 144 [PDF 169]: "no scholar actually bothered to examine the actual document until I came to the scene", and only Guillaume "bothered to check the catalog". p. 153 [PDF 178]: Renaudot "actually went to the Bibliothèque nationale, where he examined the original manuscript" and "left notes to this effect on the manuscript itself". p. 144 also lists "A note by Renaudot" in the catalogue.
5. **Folio numbers of the covenant within Arabe 214.** "folios 245-246" and "12° (Fol. 245)" (de Slane/Guillaume, p. 144) vs. "F. 257-261" (BnF index, p. 145) vs. "sheets 257 to 262, that is, the last eleven pages" (p. 146) vs. "the last folio" (p. 168). The catalogue also gives "262 fol. (f. 261 v., 262 v left blank)" and puts the copy-completion colophon at "f. 256 v".
6. **Coptic-era conversions in the catalogue data.** The book equates 1254 of the Martyrs with 1538 CE and 1336 of the Martyrs with 1620 CE (both an offset of 284), but 1380 of the Martyrs with 1592 CE (an offset of 212). On the first two offsets, 1380 would not be 1592. The arithmetic uses only the book's own equivalences. p. 144–145 [PDF 169–170]
7. **The 1673 reading mark and Vansleb.** The author argues the manuscript was with Sionita in Paris by 1630, so Vansleb could not have bought it in Egypt in 1671/1672 (p. 145). The same catalogue record lists a reading mark of Butrus ibn Diyab al-Halabi "dated 1673 CE" and says "Purchased by Vansleb in the Orient", with Vansleb seals. The author does not reconcile the 1673 mark with his chronology. pp. 144–145 [PDF 169–170]
8. **Who altered the scribe's name.** p. 147 [PDF 172]: "possible that the Sultan [Selim I] himself modified the name of the scribe"; "could have been perpetrated by the Ottomans, but most probably by the Umayyads"; or Mu'awiyah himself forged it. p. 167 [PDF 192]: the scribe and date "are almost certainly Umayyad additions". p. 174 [PDF 199]: "Could Gabriel Sionita have changed the name of the scribe in order to appease the Ottomans". These are competing hypotheses.
9. **Status of the 4 AH date.** p. 167 [PDF 192]: the date is "almost certainly [an] Umayyad addition". Yet p. 147 [PDF 172] presents it neutrally (the same year as the Assyrian covenant, a time of diplomacy), and p. 159 [PDF 184] says Hinckelmann's use of the 4 AH date "provides some important insight into the timing of the covenant".
10. **CE equivalent of 4 AH.** 626 CE (Basnage/Hinckelmann, p. 158) vs. 625 CE (Twiss, pp. 159–160). Both are reported without comment.
11. **Witness counts.** 1630 version: thirty-six (p. 147); 1690 Hamburg edition per Basnage: thirty-three (p. 158); 1538: thirty (p. 173), and catalogue "approximately thirty" (p. 144). The Najran covenant: thirty (p. 147). Marana's spy names "four main legal authorities" but lists three (Abu Bakr, 'Uthman, 'Ali) (p. 160).
12. **James Thayer Addison and Bayle.** p. 153 [PDF 178] lists "James Thayer Addison (1887-1953 CE)" among those who treat the covenant as factual, then says "A detailed defense of the covenant was also made by Dr. Addison whose arguments were summarized by Bayle". Bayle is dated 1647-1706 in the same paragraph block. The summary itself presents the covenant as one of "Mahomet's artifices", later reversed. p. 154 calls "the views of Dr. Addison ... evidently incorrect". p. 157 reports that Addison's Arabic-skilled informant judged the treaty spurious and likely forged by Scaliger. So Addison is a "defender" in one place and a sceptic in others, and the dates make Bayle summarising a 1887–1953 Addison impossible on the book's own figures.
13. **Ricaut's position.** p. 140: Ricaut "himself clearly had doubts". p. 153: Ricaut (via Bayle) "is of the same opinion with Salmasius" that it is genuine. p. 161: Ricaut "claims that 'the Turks deny that this Treaty was made'".
14. **Ricaut's translation language.** p. 153–154: Miltitz used "the French translation of the English translation of the original Arabic that had been included in Ricaut's ... Histoire". p. 154: Van Dyke's translation "was made on the basis of the French translation found in Ricaut". p. 151: it "appeared in French translations in historical works, such as Sir Paul Ricaut's ... History of the Ottoman Empire". Whether Ricaut's text was English or French is left unclear.
15. **The Ottoman printing ban vs. the Ottoman press.** p. 148 [PDF 173]: printing banned "from 1483 to 1729 CE", but Ibrahim Muteferrika "founded the Ottoman printing press in 1726" (same page).
16. **Date of Scaliger's Levant stay vs. the edicts.** p. 141: Scaliger "headed to Syria and Mount Lebanon in 1628", and came across the covenant "between 1627 and 1628". p. 142: Murad IV issued the Capuchin edicts "in April of 1627", "the time when Scaliger ... obtained permission". The 1627 date precedes the stated 1628 arrival.
17. **Scaliger's description vs. the document's date.** Scaliger says the Prophet made it "before dying" (p. 141 [PDF 166]), but the 1630 text is dated the fourth year after the hijrah (p. 147). The author does not comment.
18. **Translations of the Arabic title.** "...Concluded with the Christian Community" (pp. 139, 149) vs. "...Concluded with His Christian Subjects" (p. 146).
19. **Who published the 1630 edition.** "published by Gabriel Sionita" (Fortescue, p. 139; Layard, p. 151; the author, pp. 143, 146, 167) vs. "Published in Paris by Antoine Vitray" (p. 146) vs. "edited ... by Antoine Vitray by Louis XIII with the collaboration of Gabriel Sionite" (Balagna, p. 149). These may be compatible (printer vs. editor), but the attribution varies.
20. **Order of the republication years.** Printed as "1655, 1638, 1668, 1690, and as late as 1888" (not chronological). p. 151 [PDF 176]
21. **Salmasius's dates.** "d. 1653 CE" (p. 150) vs. "1588 or 1596-1653 CE" (p. 152). Consistent on death, uncertain on birth.
22. **Marana's dates vs. publication.** Marana "(1642-1693 CE)"; "This fourth volume ... was published in 1697". p. 160 [PDF 185]. Posthumous publication is not stated.
23. **Ends "differ substantially" vs. "almost identical".** p. 168: "both the beginning and the end differ substantially". p. 173: "The final warnings found in the covenant of 1538 are almost identical to those found in 1630". Probably the colophon and witness material vs. the warnings, but the wording is in tension.
24. **The 1630 source said to come from Mount Carmel, yet the conclusion says "Maronites from Palestine".** p. 176. Earlier the Mount Carmel provenance is disputed (p. 140), then partly accepted ("could in fact be correct", p. 142). The 1630 text is assigned to "the Maronites from Palestine" without further argument.
25. **Scaliger and French Guiana.** The friar's description says Scaliger joined "the Capuchins of the province of French Guiana" (p. 143), while the biography has him go to French Guiana only at the end of his life and die on the voyage in 1648 (p. 141).
26. **Persecution as a motive.** The author suggests Christians produced the covenant in response to the 1628 persecution (p. 141). He also says Christians under the Ottomans "had never had it better" (p. 143), and that the 1538 copy was "clearly not crafted during a period of persecution" (p. 175).
