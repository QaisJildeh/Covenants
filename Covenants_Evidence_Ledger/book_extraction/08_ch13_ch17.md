## 1. Section summary

### Chapter 13, "Examining the Authorities" (pp. 323–330 [348–355])
Morrow examines the lists of witnesses ("authorities") attached to the covenants. He says they can be read three ways: as independent chains, as offshoots of one source chain, or as late additions that do not affect the content. He goes through the witnesses on the Covenant with the Monks of Mount Sinai one by one, giving death or life dates, Sunni and Shi'i reputations, and possible corrections of misread names (Abu Hurayrah as Abu Hudayfah; Harith ibn Thabit as Hassan ibn Thabit; Abu Hanifah as Abu Hudayfah). He concludes it is a Sunni chain, inconsistent with Shi'i standards, and "relatively sound." He then examines the lists on the Covenants with the Christians of the World (1630 and 1538), Najran and Assyria. Many of these witnesses are problematic: some died before the documents could have been issued (Hamzah at Uhud, Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, Ja'far), some converted late (Mu'awiyyah, Abu al-'Aliyyah), and some are obscure early emigrants to Abyssinia. To reconcile early and late witnesses, he proposes that the Prophet re-issued the covenants regularly, perhaps yearly, or dictated them all in the Year of Delegations (630–631 CE). He applies hadith-science principles (consensus, "when in doubt, leave it out," Ibn Qayyim's content-based rejection criteria via Azami). He argues that the absence of developed isnads is a sign of antiquity, and that the covenants are mutawatir in wording and meaning (though not by chain), mashhur, marfu', musnad and muttasil. He adds that as written prophetic documents they are not subject to isnad rules, and places them in a category comparable to hadith qudsi. Variants are explained through the four modes of quotation in hadith literature (Azami 75–78).

### Chapter 14, "Tracking the Transmissions" (pp. 331–332 [356–357])
Morrow sets out four possible modes of transmission:
1. Multiple individual prophetic transmissions.
2. A single source with later segmentation, where the Najran charter (the oldest surviving, 9th century CE) is copied by others.
3. A single source with bifurcation: Najran to Egypt and the Levant; Egypt (Cairo) to Sinai, then to the Armenians of Persia, then to the Assyrians.
4. A lost "Q source" covenant, by analogy with Bible studies.

He notes that a chronology built on the dates of surviving copies is flawed. He favours the first hypothesis: most covenants are unique and were issued by the Prophet to different denominations. He leaves open how the 1538 and 1630 Covenants with the Christians of the World are related, suggesting the 1630 copy (supposedly found at Mount Carmel) was for the Levant and the 1538 copy for Egypt.

### Chapter 15, "The Covenants in Context" (pp. 333–352 [358–377])
This is a long polemical and historical survey arguing that Islam has historically treated Christians with tolerance compared with how Christians and other powers treated the conquered. It opens with anti-Muslim slurs and a Bukhari hadith against oppressing Jews and Christians. It claims the Prophet fought 68 campaigns with no more than 1,000 casualties in total, and that all his wars were just. Topics in order:
- **Early conquests:** 'Umar's restraint and garrison towns; Glubb, Donner, Brockopp, Berkey and Fletcher on the absence of destruction and on Christian growth; Nestorian sources (Yeshuyab III, the Bishop of Adiabene, John bar Penkaye).
- **Early caliphs:** Abu Bakr's renewal of the Najran covenant (Abu Yusuf); 'Umar's Jerusalem assurances; the genuine "Treaty of 'Umar" (al-Tabari, 15 AH) set against the "Pact of 'Umar" (al-Turtushi), which Morrow treats as a forgery, possibly of 'Umar II's era; 'Umar's 638 CE (17 AH) covenant in the Chronicle of Seert, and Addai Scher's view that it is apocryphal; Khalid ibn al-Walid's promise.
- **Mu'awiyyah's Christian ties:** his navy and the Battle of the Masts (655 CE); Christian courtiers; Christian imagery on early Islamic coins.
- **Contrast with other powers:** Romans (Joshel's figures), Huns, Mongols, Sassanids (614), Charlemagne (782), the Sui, Arian Visigoths.
- **Al-Andalus:** the golden age; the fall of Granada (1492), which he blames on Muslim failure to obey God and on ignoring the Prophet's succession orders; the broken Capitulation of 1492; the persecution and expulsion of the Moriscos, dated year by year (1499–1614), with casualty and expulsion figures.
- **Ottomans:** Mehmet II's Athos guarantees and Bosnian ahidname (1463); millet autonomy; the Mamluk–French treaty (1500); exceptions (Selim II, Selim I); the Selim I forced-conversion episodes (Eliot, Lybyer, Runciman, Gradeva); the Capitulations (1517–1923); the Ottoman Empire as a refuge.
- **Law and civilization:** the shari'ah as inspiration for the Napoleonic Code; a balancing passage crediting the Catholic Church and Byzantium; the distinction between traditional shari'ah and Wahhabi/Salafi practice.
- **Close:** the Chronicle of Seert's praise of Muslim rule, and a Qur'an epigraph (9:12) on p. 352.

### Chapter 16, "General Conclusions" (pp. 353–364 [378–389])
Morrow concludes:
- **Neglect:** the covenants were passed down from Islam's earliest days but ignored, which he blames on imperialists and Muslim scholars.
- **Authenticity:** their content fully agrees with Islam, and chains are not required for primary sources.
- **Witnesses:** the lists were probably appended centuries later to satisfy hadith scholars. The Persian covenant has no signatories and may predate all the others.
- **Rebuttals:**
  - Sir Paul Rycaut's view that the covenant was a ploy of a weak early community.
  - The view that the short treaties in Ibn Sa'd were inflated by Christians. Morrow answers that Ibn Sa'd's treaties are summaries, and points to dating: the Najran covenant was found in 878/879 CE, the Chronicle of Seert dates to the 9th century, and the covenants are contemporaneous with Bukhari, Muslim and Nisa'i.
  - Qureishi's rejection of the Treaty of Maqnah: Qureishi assigns it to 'Abbasid Jews under al-Mutawakkil (847–861), but Ibn Sa'd died in 845.
  - Mouton's view that the Saint Catherine covenant reflects an Islam invented by monks.
- **Politics and law:** he argues that the Prophet founded a state, and that Islam is political and a "democratic theocracy" that is not liberal democracy. This includes critiques of US politics (Carter, Bush, Obama, TARP) and Wahhabism, and praise of Shi'i ijtihad.
- **Forgery:** the only forged prophetic letter of protection is the Jewish Cairo Geniza document.
- **Critics of tradition:** he attacks Orientalist scepticism, citing Brown, Nasr, Saleh, Hawting and Cremo & Thompson. He quotes Pillsbury Dodge and Anton Haddad (who translated the Sinai covenant in 1902).
- **Closing warning:** prophetic warnings that breaking covenants brings calamity, which he says explains the Muslims' loss of power and colonization. Qur'an verses on keeping covenants follow, and an epigraph (3:199) on p. 364.

### Chapter 17, "Suggestions for Future Scholarship" (pp. 365–376 [390–401])
Morrow calls his book a thesis and an "essay," and states his conclusion that the covenants are genuine. He urges Muslim scholars to keep the thousand-year consensus on the Sinai covenant and to use ihtiyat (precaution) for the Najran and World covenants. His proposed research includes:
- Notes and fatwas on the Sinai copies, with the history of their English and French translations.
- A comparative study of Gabriel Sionita's and J. G. Nissel's editions.
- The origin of the 1538 manuscript (MS Arabe 214, BnF): its owner, scribe and named persons, the 1592 missionary, the different handwriting of its final pages, and "Michel."
- Possible links between Sionita and Cyril Lucaris.
- Archival searches in Turkey, plus reported copies at Deir-Saferan (1850), an Armenian pact (1822), a Baghdad "Traité" (1822) and Nallino's charter (1899). He also calls for searching out Leon Arpee's Persian covenant.
- Scientific dating, and even DNA testing of relics.
- Muslim communities' correspondence. His example is the Regraga Seven Saints tradition of Morocco.
- Comparison with Ottoman ahdname diplomatics.
- Textual instability arising from early Arabic script, with the Qur'an protected by memorization.
- Linguistic and dating analysis of the Arabic and Persian covenants.
- The varying witness counts (16, 22, 32, 37–38).
- The semantic narrowing of mu'min, muslim, mushrik, kafir and munafiq.
- Surveys of travel, diyarat and geographical literature.
- New translations.

The section ends with a Qur'an epigraph (2:62, p. 374), the "IV Backmatter" part title (p. 375) and an epigraph (3:77–78, p. 376).

### C. Hadith methodology applied (Ch. 13)
54. Hadith rule cited: when two hadith of equal strength contradict (halal vs. haram), "the permissible prevails over the prohibitive." p. 328 [353] [fact]
55. A tradition is not false merely because one authority is untrustworthy "if it was confirmed by trustworthy authorities who heard it in person." p. 328 [353] [fact]

### F. Pact of 'Umar vs. Treaty of 'Umar (Ch. 15)
89. The Pact of 'Umar takes "the form of a letter written by a Christian community to the Second Caliph" and is found in al-Turtushi's *Siraj al-muluk*. Its terms include: no new monasteries, churches, cells or hermitages; no repairs; three nights' lodging for Muslim travellers; no teaching the Qur'an to children; no imitating Muslim dress; no saddles or swords; no Arabic inscriptions on rings; no wine sales; shaving the front of the head; girdles; no crosses displayed; no clappers; no loud services; no palm branches or images in procession; no taking slaves who had been in Muslim possession; no striking a Muslim. pp. 336 [361] [attr: qtd. Marcus 13-15]
90. The Pact (also "'Ahd 'Umar") is dismissed as a forgery by Sunni and Shi'ite scholars Zaid Shakir, Yusuf al-Qaradawi, Maher Abu Munshar and Abdulaziz Sachedina. p. 337 [362] [attr: Danios]
91. It is disputed by Western scholars Rabbi Abraham P. Bloch, M. J. de Goeje, Leone Caetani and A. S. Tritton. p. 337 [362] [attr: Danios, Jewish Virtual Library]
92. "the so-called Covenant of 'Umar" "cannot conceivably be authentic" as it was "composed in its definitive form ca. 1100." p. 337 [362] [attr: Humphreys 257]
93. "'Umar was known for his tolerant and friendly attitude toward the protected subjects who subordinated themselves to him." p. 337 [362] [attr: Jewish Virtual Library]
94. Terms of the Treaty of 'Umar (al-Tabari): protection of lives, property, church and crosses; churches not occupied or demolished; no coercion in religion; "None of the Jews shall reside with them in Ilia'"; jizyah "as inhabitants of cities do"; "They shall evict all Romans and thieves"; free departure with the Romans; "Nothing shall be taken from them after their crops have been harvested." p. 337 [362] [attr: qtd. Isseroff]
95. The 638 CE covenant (Chronicle of Seert) grants "permanent protection" to the "inhabitants of Selenia and Nahr Bir," their Catholicos, priests and deacons. Its terms: no deposing of bishops; no destruction of churches; no use of buildings for mosques; travellers unmolested; no obligation to fight; no forced conversion (Q 2:256, 29:46). Conditions: no spying, harbouring enemies, supplying weapons, horses or men, or guiding the enemy; Christians must hide a Muslim refugee. pp. 338–339 [363–364] [attr: qtd. Scher 201-203 / 621-623]
96. Scher: the edict "appears to be a summary of the covenant attributed to Muhammad" and "is therefore probably apocryphal." Also: "these two false documents contain clauses from the act of capitulation of Jerusalem, which were renewed in the status of Syrian Christians redacted by the Caliph 'Umar." p. 339 [364] [attr: Scher 303/623]
97. "It is clear to any critical mind that the notorious Pact of 'Umar and the Treaty of 'Umar were not written by the same person." p. 339 [364] [inf/op]

## 6. The author's key arguments, conclusions and recommendations

### Chapter 13
1. The witness lists can be treated as independent chains, as offshoots of one stem, or as late additions that "in no way compromise or undermine the content." p. 323 [348]
2. The Sinai covenant's chain is Sunni, "relatively sound," and rules out a Shi'i forgery. p. 324 [349]

5. The lists are "self-correcting" when compared: a correct reading (Abu Hudayfah) cancels an impossible one (Abu Hanifah). Using consensus and dropping disputed names gives "reasonably good references." p. 328 [353]
6. "The issue of dating is really a modern Western preoccupation. The hadith literature is generally undated." p. 327 [352]
7. The covenants "should be judged primarily on the basis of their content." Early Muslim acceptance, compatibility with the shari'ah and the arguments against Christian forgery "should in themselves be sufficient evidence for their legitimacy." p. 328 [353]
8. The absence of developed isnads is "a sign of antiquity." The covenants pass every content criterion for rejecting hadith; as written, dictated documents (or as revelation) they are outside isnad rules. Variants reflect the recognized modes of quotation. pp. 329–330 [354–355]
9. "The study of the covenants… is in its infancy. The suggestions… are hypothetical, not definitive." p. 328 [353]

## 7. Internal inconsistencies within this section

Only discrepancies visible in the text itself.

1. **The Prophet's dates.** "the Prophet (570-642 CE)" on p. 339 [364]; "the Prophet Muhammad (570-632 CE)" on p. 354 [379]. The p. 339 figure also conflicts with the section's own statement that Abu Bakr (c. 573-634 CE) succeeded the Prophet as First Caliph (pp. 324, 335). Possibly a typo or OCR error.
2. **Yusuf al-Qaradawi's birth year.** "b. 1956 CE" on p. 337 [362]; "b. 1926 CE" on p. 372 [397].
3. **John of Damascus.** "(c. 645 or 676-749 CE)" on p. 340 [365]; "(d. 749/764 CE)" on p. 371 [396].
4. **Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah.** Printed "(1292/93-1286 CE)" on p. 355 [380]: the death year precedes the birth year.
5. **'Umar in Jerusalem in 659 CE.** 'Umar's dates are given as 579-644 CE (pp. 323, 334, 335, 338), but p. 335 [360] says Patriarch Solonio showed 'Umar the holy sites "in 659 CE." The same page dates Mu'awiyyah becoming "king" in Jerusalem to 659. The Ilia' treaty is dated 15 AH (p. 337) and 'Umar's Seert covenant 638 CE/17 AH (p. 338).
6. **Battle of the Masts.** Mu'awiyyah organized the navy "while governor of Syria under the Caliph 'Umar," and it "resulted in" victory at the Battle of the Masts in 655 CE (p. 340 [365]). By the book's own dates 'Umar died in 644. This is a timing tension rather than a strict contradiction.
7. **Witness counts across chapters.**
   - Ch. 13 says Sinai lists range from 22 to "thirty-seven to thirty-eight" and 16 (p. 323 [348]).
   - Ch. 17 says Sinai copies have 16 or 22, and assigns "thirty-seven or thirty-eight" to the two CW versions and Najran (p. 370 [395]).
   - Ch. 17 also calls the Assyrian list of thirty-two "One of the longest lists," though 32 is below the 37–38 cited in the same paragraph.
8. **'Ali's presence on the second group of covenants.** He is "found on two of the covenants, with the exclusion of the covenant with the Christians of the World from 1630" (pp. 324–325 [349–350]). Naming only 1630 as the exclusion implies he is on the other four, which conflicts with "two."
9. **'Abd Allah ibn 'Amr ibn al-'As.** "(d. 684 CE)" on p. 324 [349]. On p. 326 [351] "the son of 'Amr ibn al-'As (c. 592-664 CE)": if those dates are meant for 'Abd Allah rather than his father, they conflict with d. 684.
10. **Counting slip.** "Hashim ibn 'Asiyyah is found on three but identified as Hashim ibn 'Abd Allah on a third" (p. 326 [351]).
11. **Which covenant is oldest.**
    - Ch. 14 says the Najran charter (9th c.) is "the oldest such charter" (p. 331 [356]).
    - Its third hypothesis makes the Persian (Armenian) covenant a copy of the Sinai achtiname (p. 331).
    - Ch. 16 says the Persian covenant's lack of signatories suggests "perhaps, that it predates all of the others" (p. 353 [378]).
12. **Status of the witness lists.**
    - Ch. 13 treats the Sinai chain as "relatively sound," uses witnesses to date the covenants, and classes the covenants as "musnad… muttasil" (pp. 324, 329).
    - Ch. 16 concludes that the lists "were appended to the covenants centuries after the fact" (p. 353 [378]).
    - Ch. 17 says that if the chains were later forgeries "it would show that early Muslim scholars did not treat the hadith sciences very seriously" (p. 370 [395]).
13. **Relationship of the 1538 and 1630 covenants.**
    - p. 327 [352] calls the 1538 covenant the "earlier incarnation" of the 1630 covenant.
    - p. 332 [357] says "the case has yet to be closed" and suggests 1630 was for the Levant and 1538 for Egypt, i.e. separate destinations.
    - p. 366 [391] says the CW "appears to derive from" the 1538 covenant.
14. **Conquest.** p. 333 [358]: the Prophet "fought solely in the defense of Truth, and never to conquer nations." p. 356 [381]: "The Prophet did not simply raid, he conquered, and where he conquered he appointed governors."
15. **Same saying, two collections.** "Whoever oppresses a Jew or a Christian I will testify against him on the Day of Judgment" is attributed to Sahih Bukhari (p. 333 [358]). "Whoever oppresses non-Muslim subjects shall find me to be their advocate on the Day of Judgment" is attributed to Abu Dawud (p. 362 [387]). These may be distinct reports; flagged for checking.
16. **Same verse wording, two references.** "Those who believe… Jewish scriptures… the Christians, the Sabians… Last Day…" is cited as "(5:72)" on p. 341 [366] and as "(2:62)" on p. 374 [399]. The Qur'an may contain two such verses; flagged for checking.
17. **When non-Muslim rights were first encroached.** p. 349 [374] says "It was only with the establishment of a secular Turkish state under Kemal Ataturk… in 1923 that the rights of non-Muslims began to be encroached upon." The preceding pages describe earlier encroachments: Selim II's confiscation of monastic estates, Selim I's forced-conversion order, and the plundering of the Bulgarian convent (pp. 347–348). The Karabell quotation on the same page also puts the turn for the worse at the empire's fraying and collapse in the early 20th century.
18. **Who chose the first caliph.** Abu Bakr's "election" is "limited to a small circle of individuals… which excluded the participation of other parties" (p. 358 [383]). The same page says "The people, under the first four Caliphs, had a voice and a vote." Chapter 13 (p. 324) also describes the Saqifah as appointing Abu Bakr "without consulting with the community in any form or fashion."
19. **Morisco expulsion totals.** "A total of 300,000 Moriscos… were expelled… during this second wave" (p. 345) sits beside Chejne's figures summing to "about half a million" (p. 345 [370]). Presented as alternative calculations, not reconciled.
20. **Minor imprecisions.**
    - The Najran covenant found in 878/879 CE is described as "only rediscovered a couple of decades after the death of Ibn Sa'd (784-845 CE)," a gap of 33–34 years; the "Treaty of Najran… had only recently been published" (p. 354 [379]).
    - "Over five decades later, in 1899," refers back to 1822, a gap of 77 years (p. 367 [392]).
21. **Name spellings.**
    - "Qureishi" (Qureishi 182, 348–351) and "Muhammad Siddique Qureshi"/"qtd. Qureshi 30" both appear (pp. 355–356).
    - "Felipe II" and "Philip II" are both used, with the same dates (p. 344).
    - "Suhayl ibn Bayda'" also appears as "Shuhayl ibn Wahb ibn Rabiah" (pp. 325–327).
    - "Abu Hudayfah ibn Utbah" also appears as "Abu Hudayfah ibn al-'Utbah" (pp. 325, 327).
    - Fortescue appears as "Fortuescue" (p. 335) and "Fortescue" (p. 340).
    - Seert appears as "Stert" (p. 338) and "Seert" (pp. 351, 354).
