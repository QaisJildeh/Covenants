## 1. Section summary

### Chapter 6: The Prophet Muhammad and the Assyrian Christians (pp. 177–190)
1. The chapter opens by saying that the Covenant with the Assyrian Christians, unlike the Najran covenant, "has long been known" to Muslims of upper Mesopotamia. It was allegedly first composed in **Persian** (Brevick 114). The author says this should not prompt "a priori claims of forgery".
2. Following George David Malech's *History of the Syrian Nation*, the covenant was granted to "Said, head of the Nazarene tribe" and his bishop "Jahb Alahah". In 1517 Patriarch "Shimon the fourth" showed it to Sultan Selim I in Baghdad. Selim took the original to the treasury in Constantinople and gave the patriarch a copy signed by himself.
3. The author takes on suspicions of forgery or "appropriation" of the Sinai covenant: similar names to the Najran delegation, the same 1517/Selim I story as Sinai, the Persian language, a text that calls itself "The translation", 27 witnesses against 16 or 22 for Sinai, and Mu'awiyyah ibn Abi Sufyan as scribe two years before he is said to have become the Prophet's scribe (630–632). He offers alternative explanations for each.
4. He collects testimony that a firman of the Prophet was held by the Assyrian Patriarchal House. The witnesses are Wigram, Brigadier General J.G. Browne, Surma D'Bait Mar Shimum, Earl Percy, Stafford, Emhardt & Lamsa, Theodore D'Mar Shimun, Yohannan, Albert Yelda (via Siemon-Netto), and Bar Hebraeus via Assemani via Percy.
5. He sets out the conflicting accounts of the relic's fate:
   - destroyed in Bedr Khan Beg's massacres in 1847 (Stafford, Browne), or perishing in the mid-19th century (Wigram);
   - stolen in 1845 (Surma), or stolen at Dizan and taken to Bedr Khan (Theodore);
   - carried off by Turkish troops in 1899 (Percy);
   - destroyed by Turkish rulers before a massacre of 30,000 (Yelda). The author reads this as the 1915 Assyrian Genocide.

   He speculates it may survive in private hands.
6. He gives a biography of Bedir (Badr) Khan Beg (c. 1800–1868). He argues that Kurdish and Turkish attacks on Assyrians were nationalist and secular, not Islamic, and that the attackers "had to effectively apostatize".
7. A long "Commentary on the Content of the Covenant" goes item by item through:
   - protection of the "Nazarene nation", churches and property;
   - negotiable tribute paid into the bayt al-mal (1 dinar for commoners, 12 for the rich; nothing from strangers or the propertyless; a tax on inheritance);
   - no forced conversion, rights of Christian women and wives, and Muslim help in building churches;
   - Christian duties: no shelter for enemies, three days' hospitality, protection of Muslim women and children;
   - forfeiture of the treaty on breach, and entrusting the document to the head of the church.
8. The commentary carries long modern digressions, among them the Bamiyan Buddhas (2001), Saudi and Iraqi edicts of 2012, Mehmet II and Hagia Sophia (1453/1935), and the Ka'bah icons of Jesus and Mary. The author claims that many militant Muslim movements are controlled by Western and Israeli intelligence services.
9. The Conclusions say the Assyrian, Sinai and Najran covenants are "completely compatible" and "the work of the same genius". They show Muhammad as statesman and diplomat, and the author rejects the view that the spread of Islam was imperialism.

### Chapter 7: The Prophet Muhammad and the Armenian Christians of Jerusalem (pp. 191–202)
1. The author places the Armenian covenant in a "concerted campaign" by Muhammad. In his inference, the aim was to build a buffer zone of Christian sympathizers against Byzantium and the Latins.
2. The document, "Firman Attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, Confirming the Rights of the Armenians in the Holy Places of Jerusalem", is a paper-on-silk scroll measuring 327 x 21.5 cm. It is held in the St. James Library of the Armenian Patriarchate of Jerusalem and is little known.
3. Armenian sources (Azadian; Krikorian via Hagopian; Belleville Armenian Church citing Zeki al-Din) say Patriarch Abraham I (638–669) went to Mecca, or in 626 went to Muhammad, to obtain it. The author thinks it was most likely obtained in Medina.
4. He attacks Father Jerome Golubovich's 1921 book *I Frati Minori*, and its review in *Revue Biblique*, as biased. Golubovich called the Sinai Pact, the Treaty of 'Umar, Selim I's firman and the Armenian covenant forgeries, mostly of 1630–1634 under Murad IV. The author's rebuttal includes Jean Thenaud's 1512 sighting of the Sinai privileges.
5. He reproduces in full the Firman (Achtiname) of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab to Sophronios, dated 20 Rabi' al-Awwal, 15 AH. He also reproduces in full Johannes Avdall's 1870 English translation of the Covenant of 'Ali (Safar, 40 AH; given as 660 CE by the author).
6. His commentary on the Covenant of 'Ali points to its Shi'ite traits and argues that a Christian forger would not have produced them.
7. He cites the Charter of Caliph Muktafi II to the Nestorian Church (1138, ed. A. Mingana), Salah al-Din's firman (1187 per Dadoyan), and the Armenian historians Samuel of Ani, Mxit'ar of Ani, Kirakos and Juanser. He sets the doubts of Sanjian, Dadoyan and R.W. Thomson against them.
8. He argues for an "unbroken chain" or "covenantal continuity" from the Prophet through 'Umar, 'Ali, the Umayyads, Muktafi II and Salah al-Din to the 1917 edict of Sharif al-Husayn ibn 'Ali of Mecca.
9. A polemical passage follows against "Takfiris", Wahhabis, Saudi Arabia and their "Western backers".
10. The Conclusions note that the Patriarchate cautiously says "attributed to" and keeps the relic from public view. The author states his belief that it and the other covenants are "most likely" genuine and asks the Patriarchate to release it for study.
11. Part II then begins: a divider page (p. 203) and an epigraph, Qur'an 3:3–4 (p. 204).

## 4. Factual claims (non-date), by theme

Basis tags: [Fact] = author states as fact; [Attr. X] = attributed to source X; [Trad.] = tradition; [Inf.] = author's inference; [Op.] = author's opinion.

### A. Chapter 6: Origin, language and transmission of the Assyrian covenant
1. Unlike the Najran covenant, unknown to Muslims until its rediscovery "almost one hundred years ago", the Assyrian covenant "has long been known to the Muslims from upper Mesopotamia or what is now southeastern Turkey and part of northern Iraq". p. 177 [PDF 202] [Fact]
2. "Unlike most of the other covenants of the Prophet, the original ... was allegedly composed in the Persian language." p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. Brevick 114]
3. Malech calls it the "Agreement between Prophet Mohammed and Nazarene Christians of the East". p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. Malech]
4. "Mohammed wrote an epistle to Said, head of the Nazarene tribe, who were Christians, and asked him and his people to accept the teachings of Islam. Together [with] Jahb Alahah, their bishop, Said appeared before the Prophet and agreed to pay him tribute for enjoying the liberty to worship in their own way unharmed by the Islamites." p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. Malech 221]
5. "The document was signed by Mohammed himself and a number of the leading Islamites. It is written in the Persian language and has been preserved among the Christians for many centuries." p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. Malech 221]
6. The original was preserved by the Assyrian Christians until the time of Selim I, "only to be seized by the Sultan". p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. "is said"; Malech]
7. Selim said the document "was so valuable, that it ought to be preserved in the royal treasury of Constantinople". p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. Malech 221]
8. "Jahb Alahah may be a corruption of Abu Haritha while Said could be the Sayyid 'Absiso, the leaders of the delegation from Najran." p. 177 [PDF 202] [Inf.]
9. "For some scholars", the shared characteristics with the Sinai covenant "may point to appropriation". The Assyrians may have copied the Sinai covenant, addressed to "all Christians", and come to believe it was their own; "This remains within the realm of possibility." p. 177 [PDF 202] [Attr. unnamed "some scholars"; Inf.]
10. "The repertoire of personal names among the Christians of the time was very limited in scope." p. 178 [PDF 203] [Fact/Op.]
11. "Of all the Caliphs, Selim I was of the most relentless in his search for religious relics." He and his agents sought ancient Qur'ans, letters of the Prophet, and items of the Prophet, his Companions or earlier prophets. p. 178 [PDF 203] [Fact]
12. Conquered communities "often approach their new rulers to express their submission ... and to request protection or privileges". 1517 "would have been the ideal time for the Assyrian or Armenian Christians to show their copies". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Inf.]
13. "Traditions in Muslim and Bukhari" demonstrate that the Prophet could speak Persian. p. 178 [PDF 203] [Attr. hadith collections, as characterized by the author]
14. Salman al-Farsi "was perfectly proficient, not only in Persian but in Aramaic, and possibly Greek"; he "was not only literate". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Fact]
15. The Persian language "does not appear to have surprised Selim I or any of his scholars". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Inf.]
16. Had the Ottomans thought it a fake, it would have been destroyed and the forgers "put to the sword". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Inf.]
17. The document "commences with the words: 'The translation of the holy messenger's treaty, peace be upon him and his family.'" This initial section, in Malech's version, was not translated into English. p. 178 [PDF 203] [Fact]
18. The scribe "is said to have been Mu'awiyyah ibn Abi Sufyan". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Attr. "is said"]
19. Several native Persian-speaking professors of religious studies judged the language consistent with 17th-century Persia. One felt it "so archaic that it could conceivably have been written during the time of the Prophet". None saw any sign of forgery. p. 178 [PDF 203] [Attr. unnamed colleagues]
20. "It has not yet been established whether the version in Malech's text was transcribed in modern or ancient times." p. 178 [PDF 203] [Fact]
21. The English translation "is good" but "not perfectly accurate, particularly when it comes to the list of witnesses at the end". p. 178 [PDF 203] [Attr. "I am told"]
22. The Persian covenant has 27 witnesses. "Two of the Sinai covenants have sixteen signatories and four have twenty-two." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Fact]
23. "Muttawatir" (printed so) is the highest hadith grade, meaning passed down by many narrators. p. 179 [PDF 204] [Fact]
24. "Assyrian tradition declares that special privileges were given to their church and their patriarch, by a firman of the Prophet himself." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Wigram, *An Introduction* 310]
25. "[T]he Prophet Mohammed ... gave the Assyrians a written firman ... according them permission to carry out their own religious observances without interference." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Browne, n.p.]
26. "It is a thing of common knowledge among the Mussalmans of Hakkiari that there was a special firman granted by the Prophet to our house, and therewith ... a certain special knife of his giving." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Surma, n.p.]
27. "The strictest Moslems ... will eat without hesitation of any animal slaughtered by a member of the Patriarchal family." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Surma, n.p.]
28. "The patriarchal house ... is regarded with a respect among the Moslems, which they do not show to other Christian bishops." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Wigram, *An Introduction* 310]
29. In Jelu, among the relics of the great church of Mar Zaia was "a handkerchief covered with Arabic writing which the natives believe to be a firman of the Prophet". p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Percy 645]
30. Tradition holds that the Porte's firman to each successive Patriarch, confirming his authority over the Nestorians, was originally granted by Mahomet to Ishy Yau, then Patriarch of the East, residing at Baghdad. Assemani's *Bibliotheca Orientalis*, compiled for Clement XI, gives the Latin text of Bar Hebraeus's account. p. 179 [PDF 204] [Trad.; Attr. Percy 645–646]
31. Per Bar Hebraeus (via Percy), the treaty's terms were:
    - it was negotiated with large presents through Said, Christian Prince of Najran ("Nagranensium");
    - it was a "diploma" commending Christians to Arab protection;
    - it safeguarded religion and laws and exempted them from military service;
    - it shielded Christians in Muslim households from insults to their faith;
    - it allowed them to erect churches as they pleased, with Arab help;
    - it fixed the taxes on rich and poor "with strict and minute precision".
    p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Percy 645–646 / Bar Hebraeus]
32. The firman was "embossed in letters of gold, on parchment, and was written 'circle wise,' with the print of a hand in the middle". p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Surma, n.p.]
33. An explanatory parenthesis says this is "an ancient type of Arab script": the donor's handprint was impressed in the middle and the decree written around it in one continuous spiral. p. 179 [PDF 204] [Within the Surma quotation, n.p.]
34. "The knife had a shaft of silver, with a piece of red coral set in the pommel, and an inscription inlaid in gold on the blade." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Surma, n.p.]
35. "Once in the year a selected Mollah of Julamerk used to come up and read that document in the public assembly." p. 179 [PDF 204] [Attr. Surma, n.p.]
36. The memory of the covenant "was maintained alive among both Muslims and Christians". p. 179 [PDF 204] [Inf.]
37. "Many clerics had come to Muhammad seeking charters." p. 179–180 [PDF 204–205] [Fact/Op.]
38. "The rights confirmed by 'Umar are clearly the very same granted in the Covenant." p. 180 [PDF 205] [Op.]
39. Under Omar, Assyrian Church Christians had "full security as a 'millet'", "on practically the same terms, as they had received under the Persian". The terms:
    - the right to remain in the nationality and practice their religion;
    - exemption from military service;
    - churches could be repaired, but new ones not erected without permission;
    - ordinary taxes plus the 'kharj', in place of military service; monks exempt;
    - Christian girls married to Muslims "might keep their religion's rule".
    p. 180 [PDF 205] [Attr. Wigram, *The Assyrians* 33]
40. A copy of the firman was preserved by the Patriarch of the Assyrian Church. p. 180 [PDF 205] [Attr. Stafford 95]
41. The covenant granted the Assyrian Church freedom of religion and administration, and the right to be a millet: a protected community headed by its chief Bishop, who represented it before Muslim sovereigns. p. 180 [PDF 205] [Attr. Stafford 95]
42. The relic "was stored in the great church of Mar Zaia in Jilu, a district located in the Hakkari region of upper Mesopotamia which consisted of some twenty Assyrian Christian villages". p. 180 [PDF 205] [Fact]
43. "It is reported" that when the patent was raised, Muslims dropped their weapons and left the Assyrians unharmed. The Muslims knew the covenant's warning that the disobedient would be excluded from heaven's blessings. p. 180 [PDF 205] [Trad./Attr. "it is reported"]
44. The original "was taken to Istanbul, where, unfortunately, it has yet to be located". p. 180 [PDF 205] [Fact/Attr.]
45. "A documentary decree was issued by Mohammed and written by Ali, his son-in-law, in which he promises to the Nestorians protection and privileges ... It is now said to be in the Ottoman Museum in Constantinople." p. 180 [PDF 205] [Attr. Emhardt & Lamsa 75–76]
46. Neither the Assyrian nor the Sinai covenant is "currently in the catalog of the collection housed in the famous Turkish museum". pp. 180–181 [PDF 205–206] [Fact]
47. The Patriarchal family "make no mention of any such transfer" and indicate that their firman was the original. It also included a dagger given by the Prophet. p. 181 [PDF 206] [Attr. "the actual owners"]
48. Surma was the sister of Mar Shimun XXI Benyamin. "The sacred relics of the Prophet actually belonged to her family and she relates accounts from people who had actually seen them." p. 181 [PDF 206] [Fact]

### B. Chapter 6: Fate of the Assyrian firman
49. The grant "was actually preserved until the middle of the 19th century, when Kurdish hatred of Christians overcame their reverence for the Prophet, and the grant perished in the 'Massacres of Bedr Khan Beg'". p. 181 [PDF 206] [Attr. Wigram, *An Introduction* 310]
50. Stafford dates the event to 1847 and says the firman was destroyed. p. 181 [PDF 206] [Attr. Stafford 95]
51. Browne also gives 1847. In his account the Wali of Mosul, "frightened by the tales ... of the increasing strength of the Assyrians", organized "a political massacre". 54. Uncle Hormizd overheard Bedr Khan Beg's son in an Istanbul coffeehouse say, "Our family possess something that is invaluable among the Muslim world."    - The Covenant of 'Ali's text: "the month of Safar, in the fortieth year of the hijrah" (p. 195 [PDF 220]).
   - The author: "660 CE" (p. 194 [PDF 219]).
   - The book does not reconcile these. Dadoyan also places 'Ali's arrangement before 'Umar's ("a little later by Caliph 'Umar"), while the author's chain runs "from 'Umar to 'Ali" (p. 201).
4. **The Assyrian covenant's date against its scribe.** The covenant "dates from two years before" 630–632, but Mu'awiyyah was scribe only 630–632 (p. 183). The author acknowledges this and offers explanations. It also sits awkwardly with his earlier remark that delegations came "during his final two years of life" (p. 178).
5. **Scribe of the Assyrian covenant.** Mu'awiyyah ibn Abi Sufyan (pp. 178, 183) against "written by Ali, his son-in-law" (Emhardt & Lamsa, p. 180). The author does not address the 'Ali attribution.
6. **Recipient of the Assyrian covenant.** Said and bishop Jahb Alahah (Malech, p. 177); Ishy Yau, Patriarch at Baghdad, via Said the Prince of Najran (Percy/Bar Hebraeus, p. 179); Nestorian Patriarch Essvo Yabh, "toward the end of the seventh" [century] (Emhardt & Lamsa, p. 180). The last date is also in tension with a Prophet who died by 632 (p. 183).
7. **Language and material of the Assyrian document.** Persian (Malech; Brevick, p. 177) against "a handkerchief covered with Arabic writing" (Percy, p. 179) against gold letters "on parchment", "an ancient type of Arab script" (Surma, p. 179). The document also calls itself "The translation of the holy messenger's treaty" (p. 178). The author notes the oddity of the translation heading only.
8. **Whether the original is in Istanbul or was held by the Patriarchal family.**
   - The original was taken to Constantinople in 1517 and the patriarch kept a certified copy that is "still extant" (Malech, pp. 177, 180).
   - The family's firman was the original (Surma, as reported by the author, p. 181).
   - The family treasured it until the end of the 19th century, and it is "now said to be in the Ottoman Museum" (Emhardt & Lamsa, p. 180).
   - Two of these accounts put the original in Istanbul; Surma's puts it with the family. The author asks what happened to the certified copy (p. 180), then follows Surma.
9. **Where the relic was kept.** "Stored in the great church of Mar Zaia in Jilu" (author, p. 180; Percy, p. 179). "Kept among the treasures of the Patriarchate" (Surma, p. 181). Carried in a satchel at Dizan; "the firman stolen from Qudshanis" (Theodore, p. 181).
10. **Fate and date of loss.** Wigram: perished in the mid-19th century (p. 181). Stafford and Browne: destroyed in 1847 (p. 181). Surma: stolen in 1845 (p. 181). Theodore: stolen at Dizan (p. 181). Percy: carried off by Turkish troops in 1899 (p. 181). Yelda: disappeared in 1847, later destroyed by Turkish rulers before a massacre (p. 182).
    - The author both says Percy's date "seems off by nearly half a century" (p. 181) and that it "should not be so easily dismissed" (p. 182).
    - He earlier ranks Surma's testimony above Percy's "scholarly speculations" (p. 181).
    - Surma's "(1845)" also differs from the 1847 date the author gives for the massacre and Bedir Khan's defeat (pp. 181–182).
11. **Who counts as eyewitness.** The author calls Surma "closely connected to the events, as opposed to a distant observer" (p. 181), but gives her dates as 1883–1975 (p. 179) for events of 1845/1847. He partly allows for this ("she relates accounts from people who had actually seen them", p. 181). Theodore D'Mar Shimun (1906–2001) narrates the Dizan theft in the first person plural ("When we went to Dizan ... our men went to fight", p. 181).
12. **'Umar's terms against the covenant on church building.** The author says the rights 'Umar confirmed "are clearly the very same granted in the Covenant" (p. 180). His Wigram quotation says Christians "might repair their churches, but might not erect new ones without permission" (p. 180). The covenant, by contrast, requires Muslim neighbors to help build churches (item 11, p. 187), as does Bar Hebraeus's version (Christians "allowed to erect churches as they pleased", p. 179).
13. **Persian or Byzantine background of the Assyrians.** Wigram says the 'Umar terms matched what they "had received under the Persian" (p. 180). The author says the Prophet was dealing with "what previously was a vassal kingdom of the Byzantines" (p. 184).
14. **Military help from the Assyrian Christians.**
    - The Assyrians "committed to pay taxes ... and help the Muslims when called to do so" (p. 184).
    - They "were allowed to help the Muslims, although they were under no obligation to do so" (p. 186).
    - Item 4 says "will help the Muslims" (p. 184). Item 7 says "not obliged to make war" (p. 186). Wigram and Bar Hebraeus give exemption from military service (pp. 179–180).
15. **Item numbering in the commentary.**
    - "Item eleven" is given for two different clauses: church building (p. 187) and honoring "a great and learned man" (p. 189).
    - "Item four" covers possessions (p. 184) and, later, "churches and buildings shall be left as they are" (p. 185). "Item five" covers agreed tribute (p. 184) and churches not torn down (p. 185).
    - These may or may not be the same items; the text does not clarify. Items 3, 12 and 16 are never cited by number.
16. **The 'Umar firman is labelled "with the Armenian Christians".** The heading says "with the Armenian Christians of Jerusalem" (pp. 192–193), but the text is addressed to Sophronios, "the Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem" / "Patriarch of the Imperial Nation" (p. 193). It lists the Armenians among groups who "will belong to said Patriarch" (p. 193).
17. **Golubovich's position on the 'Umar treaty.** He listed "the Treaty of 'Umar" among the fakes (p. 192). The author then offers the Firman of 'Umar as evidence against him (p. 193) without addressing whether it is the document Golubovich called fake.
18. **The author's paraphrase of Dadoyan against his quotation of her.**
    - He says Dadoyan "recognizes that a long succession of Muslim rulers ... all renewed the edict" (p. 200). The quotation frames this as Armenian opinion ("This is how Armenians viewed ...", "In their opinion, the Umayyads simply 'renewed'", p. 200).
    - He says "Salah al-Din accepted the edicts of 'Ali and 'Umar as authentic" (p. 200). The quotation says Armenians "were said to have shown Salah ed-Din the oaths of 'Ali and 'Umar"; "accepted as authentic" refers to Salah ed-Din's own oath (p. 200).
19. **Does 'Ali's covenant confirm the Prophet's?** The author says 'Ali's decree "confirmed" the Prophet's covenant with the Armenians (p. 194). The quoted Covenant of 'Ali does not explicitly mention a prior covenant of the Prophet with the Armenians. It speaks of "a Covenant of patriarchs, of prophets, which was brought down from heaven by an angel" (p. 196). This is an observation about the quoted text only.
20. **Title against place in the Covenant of 'Ali.** The title is "Covenant of 'Ali, Fourth Caliph, of Baghdad" (p. 194). The document says it was written at "the celebrated domicile of Kharanthala" (p. 195).
21. **Mingana's quotation applied to "the Firman".** Mingana edited the Muktafi II charter (p. 198). On p. 201 his remark is quoted with the bracketed insertion "the Firman", in the context of the Sharif's 1917 edict and the chain of covenants. The referent is ambiguous.
22. **Spelling variants** that may matter for checking:
    - "Surma D'Bait Mar Shimum" (pp. 179, 181) against "Mar Shimun XXI Benyamin" and "Theodore D'Mar Shimun" (p. 181);
    - "Bedr Khan Beg" / "Bedru Khan Beg" / "Bedir Khan Beg" / "Bedir (Badr) Khan Beg";
    - "Jelu" (Percy) / "Jilu"; "Hakkiari" / "Hakkari";
    - "Mu'awiyyah" / "Muawiyah"; "Ishy Yau" / "Essvo Yabh";
    - "Salah al-Din" / "Salah ed-Din";
    - "Hagia Sofia"; "muttawatir" (printed so).
23. **Two characterizations of Salah al-Din.** "A notorious nasibi [hater of the Household of the Prophet]" yet "famous for his tolerance and nobility of character" (p. 199). The author presents both; this is a tension of characterization, not a factual contradiction.
