## 3. Discrepancies ranked by significance (most serious first)

1. **The "twenty-five years of silence" premise (pp. 6, 12) is contradicted by the sira.** This is the factual base of the chapter's central argument that Muhammad's whereabouts between ages 15 and 40 are unknown and can be filled from Sinai tradition. Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Sa'd and al-Tabari place him in Mecca at the Fijar war and the Hilf al-fudul (age 20), at the Khadijah caravan and marriage (25), and at the rebuilding of the Ka'ba, where he acted as arbiter (35). Sources: G pp. 56–57, 82, 84; IS pp. 143–148; T6 [1127], [1130], [1140]. The record is thin, and Watt notes that the ages are rounded to multiples of five (T6 p. xxii). But it is not silent.
2. **His participation in the Fijar war is called "spurious" (p. 6), and Safdar Husayn is said to "wrongly allege" it (p. 12).** Ibn Sa'd quotes the Prophet: "I attended it with my uncles and shot arrows there and I do not repent it" (IS p. 143). Ibn Hisham's note 124 (G pp. 709–710) also has him present. The book's own key sources contradict the dismissal, and the book gives no source for it.
3. **Sprenger is used selectively (p. 4).** The book cites Sprenger as support for Bahira's historicity, in the frame of "recognition, not influence". Sprenger actually argued that Bahira, "whom ancient Christian writers call Sergius," may have returned to Mecca with Muhammad (SPR p. 576). He urged weight for "the charges of the Christians" (p. 591), suggested the first journey might be "altogether apocryphal" (p. 588 n.), and valued al-Waqidi's version because it admits Muhammad "was in his youth an idolator" (p. 592). The phrase "cautiously introduced by 'it is supposed'" (book p. 3) is Sprenger's, used without attribution and in a narrower sense.
4. **Arpee's Persian covenant is dated 1948 in the front matter (xviii).** The archive.org catalogue, the book's own bibliography and its Ch. 3 all give **1946**.
5. **The marriage is dated "582 CE" (p. 12),** against 595 on p. 6 and in every source (IS p. 148; BRIT). It is an internal slip in the passage that sets up the "silence" argument.
6. **Factual errors in the Athens/Rome comparison (p. 32).** Sparta is grouped with Athenian democracy. "Most peasants" are said to be excluded from Athenian citizenship, but thetes sat in the assembly and courts (*Ath. Pol.* 7.3). Plebeians are said to have been unable to "rule, elect rulers or make use of land", but there were plebeian consuls from 366 BCE (Livy 6.42) and land-holding limits under the Licinian law (Livy 6.35). The comparison's conclusion depends on these premises.
7. **Christians are put inside the Medinan "community of believers" (p. 33).** The Constitution's text (G pp. 231–233) names Jewish groups only, never Christians. The idea that People of the Book belonged to the umma is close to Donner's thesis, which the book attacks on the same page. Critics such as Crone call that thesis conjecture beyond a few Qur'anic verses.
8. **John of Damascus is misattributed (p. 4).** He speaks of an unnamed "Arian monk". The identification with Bahira is a modern editor's footnote (orthodoxinfo.com; SPR p. 590).
9. **"No complete chain of transmission" (p. 3).** Tirmidhi 3620 does have a full isnad to Abu Musa al-Ash'ari. The book's real point, that the account is weakly graded (da'if per Darussalam; anachronism noted by SPR p. 591), is better supported than its stated reason.
10. **"Nine or fifteen years" (p. 4)** has no source support. The sources give 9 or 12, as does the book's own p. 2.
11. **Hilf al-fudul is put at 595 CE (p. 12, via Safdar Husayn).** Ibn Sa'd puts it right after Fijar at age 20, about 590 (IS p. 144). The Prophet's presence is reported in his own words (G p. 57), not merely "supposed".
12. **Qur'an 5:29 is mis-cited (p. 25).** It is the Cain and Abel verse. The intended verse is probably 5:2, on not violating the sha'a'ir (rites) of Allah.
13. **The Night Journey route is self-contradictory (p. 21 vs p. 27).** The p. 21 version matches Nasa'i 450. The p. 27 ordering (Syria, Palestine, Sinai) does not.
14. **Smaller items.** The Busra distance is 145 km (road figures are about 140 km; straight line about 112 km; Yusuf 'Ali, the book's own source, says about 70 miles). Yusuf 'Ali is framed as if he proposed Edom (he favours Syrian Busra). Aminah's "dream" is a light seen in pregnancy in Ibn Ishaq. The claim that Khadijah was one of the Hanafiyyah is not in Ibn Ishaq's list of hanifs. The Constitution is "Ibn Ishaq 233" (actually pp. 231–233). Lecker 2000 runs to pp. 157–169, not 167. Hebrews 11:1 has "substance", not "presence". "Omid Saji" is a misprint for Omid Safi. John Herlihy is missing from the online signatory list.

## 4. Assessment of the author's main arguments (neutral)

**(a) Bahira as a historical "recognition", not "influence" (pp. 2–5).**
*For the author:* The meeting appears in all the major early compilations: Ibn Ishaq (G pp. 79–81), Ibn Sa'd (IS pp. 134, 174–175), al-Tabari (T6 pp. 44–46) and al-Tirmidhi (T3620, which has an isnad). Some Western historians allow a kernel of truth: Glubb says it "may well record some meeting" (GLUBB), and Sprenger believed Bahira was historical (SPR). UNESCO's description of Bosra reflects the strength of the local memory.
*Against:* The sources disagree on basic facts. The age is 9 or 12 (T6 [1125]; IS p. 134), and the monk's name and the story's details vary. The isnad version is graded weak and contains an anachronism (T3620 grade; SPR p. 591 on Bilal). Watt, translating al-Tabari, treats the story as "primarily a way of reassuring people that Muhammad was really a prophet" (T6 p. xxix). Bell: "No credence can be given" (BELL p. 21). The book says "the sources are unanimous" that "something spiritually and politically significant took place" (p. 4), but the sources agree only on a recognition story, not on its details or historicity. And the 19th-century scholar the book leans on most (Sprenger) used the episode to argue the reverse, a possible *influence* (row 19).

**(b) The "obscure" decades (ages 15–40) and the case for a Sinai sojourn (pp. 6–15).**
*For the author:* Guillaume's introduction admits that "no such accuracy … can be claimed for the Meccan period". Watt warns that the age-markers 20, 25, 35 and 40 are rounded approximations (T6 p. xxii). So there is real uncertainty about how Muhammad spent these years, and long trade journeys are plausible. Bell accepts that he traded and knew the northern caravan route (BELL p. 21).
*Against:* The same sources do not leave the period blank. They place Muhammad in Mecca at 20 (Fijar and the Hilf al-fudul), 25 (marriage) and 35 (the Ka'ba). Bukhari 2262 has him shepherding "the sheep of the people of Mecca". A thin record is not evidence for an alternative location. The Sinai evidence itself is assessed by another agent, but the earliest critical edition the book cites (Moritz 1918) dated the Sinai document to no earlier than about 900 CE (row 76).

**(c) The Constitution of Medina as a pluralist, egalitarian charter that included Jews (and Christians) in the umma (pp. 27–33).**
*For the author:* Scholars widely accept the document as authentic (WATT-M p. 225; DON p. 72). It says the Jews of Banu 'Awf "are one community with the believers (the Jews have their religion and the Muslims have theirs)" (G p. 233). Donner reads it as evidence that "some of Medina's Jews … were recognized as being part of the umma" (DON pp. 72–73).
*Against:* Its date and unity are disputed. The main Jewish tribes are absent from it (WATT-M pp. 225–228; DON p. 73). Christians do not appear at all (G pp. 231–233). It keeps clan-based blood-money "according to their present custom" and family control over protection given to women (G pp. 232–233), so "all privileges of class were abolished" is an interpretation, not the text. The claimed superiority over Athens and Rome rests partly on factual errors about those polities (rows 61–62). Whether "never seen since" or "superior" is apt is a value judgment and cannot be checked.

**(d) The rebuttal of Donner (p. 33).**
*For the author:* The Arabic of 3:19 and 5:3 does have the definite *al-islam* (QAPI). The author is not alone in criticising Donner: Crone found his central thesis to be "conjecture" beyond a few verses (*Tablet* 2010).
*Against:* Donner's case does not rest on ignoring the article. He argues from Qur'anic usage, citing verses where *muslim* is applied to Jesus' disciples and other monotheists (DON pp. 71–72; QAPI 3:52). A definite article does not by itself decide whether *al-islam* names a confessional religion or the act of submission. Calling Donner ignorant of "basic Arabic" is polemic, not refutation. There is also a tension in the book: its own claim that Jews and Christians belonged to the "Community of Believers" in the Prophet's time (p. 33) is essentially Donner's thesis.

**(e) Use of sources generally.**
Primary quotations from Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Sa'd and Bukhari are generally accurate. The secondary literature is used more loosely: Sprenger is quoted selectively, John of Damascus is misattributed, Yusuf 'Ali is framed as if he proposed Edom, and the dismissal of the Fijar reports has no source. Several modern authorities (Roggema, Peters, Esposito, Hoyland, Crone, Lecker) could not be checked page by page (section 5).
