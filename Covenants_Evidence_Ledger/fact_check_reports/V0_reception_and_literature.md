### 2.4 The overall pattern

- **The positive reviews (R1–R5)** appeared in Islamic-studies, interfaith or regional-history venues. None of them weighs the manuscript or diplomatic evidence. At least two of the reviewers (Sellam, Martelli) were connected to Morrow's project.
- **The critical assessments** come from:
  - Reynolds, a specialist in the Qurʾān and Christian–Muslim relations;
  - two Muslim academics (Sanni, Hussain);
  - for the sequels, Valkenberg and Monferrer-Sala.
- **Peer-reviewed defences after 2013** come almost entirely from one circle:
  - El-Wakil and Zein (HBKU College of Islamic Studies);
  - Morrow himself;
  - authors in an MDPI *Religions* collection on the Prophet's letters and covenants. The special-issue page appeared only in a snippet; MDPI returned 403.
- I found **no** historian of St Catherine's or specialist in Islamic diplomatics who endorses Morrow's conclusions in a page I could open.

## 3. Other books and studies on these covenants

### 3.1 Pre-modern Muslim records of the Najran treaty vs. the long Christian "Covenant with the Christians of Najran" [SA1; key items spot-checked]

**The short treaty in the early Muslim sources.** The early Muslim sources preserve a **short, tribute-centred treaty (ṣulḥ)**. Its terms:
- **Tribute:** 2,000 robes (*ḥulla*), half in Rajab and half in Safar.
- **Loan for war in Yemen:** 30 coats of mail, 30 horses and 30 camels.
- **Hospitality:** lodging for the Prophet's envoys, up to about 20 days.
- **Protection:** "جوار الله وذمة محمد" (the protection of God and the dhimma of Muhammad) over their persons, property, religion and churches (*biyaʿ*).
- **Clergy:** no bishop or monk to be removed.
- **No army** to tread their land.
- **Riba:** usury is forbidden on pain of losing protection.

What each source contributes:
- **Abu Yusuf, *Kitāb al-Kharāj*** (shamela.ws 26333/86–92) calls his text the copy "التي في أيديهم" (which is in their hands). Witnesses: Abu Sufyan, Ghaylan b. ʿAmr, Malik b. ʿAwf, al-Aqraʿ, al-Mughira. Scribe: ʿAbd Allah b. Abi Bakr. He also reports that ʿUmar moved them to "Najran of Iraq" after they acquired horses and weapons.
- **Abu ʿUbayd, *Kitāb al-Amwāl*** nos. 517–519 (OpenITI text):
  - no. 517: a *mursal* isnad.
  - no. 518: ʿUmar expelled them because "أصابوا الربا في زمانه" (they took usury in his time). They settled at al-Najraniyya near Kufa, and ʿUthman cut 200 robes.
- **Ibn Zanjawayh, *al-Amwāl*** nos. 732–733: nearly identical to Abu ʿUbayd.
- **Ibn Saʿd, *al-Ṭabaqāt*** I:249: the same terms, adding "وبيعهم وصلواتهم" (their churches and their prayers).
- **al-Balādhurī, *Futūḥ al-buldān*** (Hitti tr. 1916, ch. XIV):
  - Yahya b. Adam's copy reads "No attempt shall be made to turn a bishop from his office…".
  - Another copy ended "Written by ʿAli ibn-abu-Talib", and Yahya was "at a loss to know what to say".
- **Abu Dawud** no. 3041: "لا تهدم لهم بيعة… ولا يفتنوا عن دينهم ما لم يحدثوا حدثا، أو يأكلوا الربا" (no church of theirs is to be demolished … nor are they to be pressed to leave their religion, so long as they commit no offence or consume usury).
- **Ibn Hishām / Ibn Isḥāq:** the delegation and the refused *mubāhala* (mutual-curse challenge), but **no treaty text**.
- **Yaḥyā b. Ādam's own surviving *Kharāj*:** no treaty text.

**The long version: *Chronicle of Seert*** (ed. Addai Scher, *Patrologia Orientalis* 13, 1919, pp. 600–623).
- **Claimed origin:** the copy was made "sur un registre qui fut retrouvé en 265 [AH = 878–79 CE] à Rirmantha chez Habib le moine". Habib said it came from the caliphal library and was written on ox-hide with Muhammad's seal. (Spot-checked.)
- **What it adds to the short treaty:**
  - fixed per-head tax rates (4 dirhams a year, 12 at most for the wealthy);
  - clergy exempt from tax;
  - "Aucun chrétien ne sera fait musulman par force" (no Christian is to be made Muslim by force);
  - no forced marriage, and a Christian wife keeps her faith;
  - Muslims to help "réparer leurs églises et leurs couvents" (repair their churches and monasteries);
  - extension to Christians "de Najran ou de toute autre secte" (of Najran or of any other sect);
  - praise for Christians who fought alongside him;
  - about 30 witnesses, ending "a écrit aussi Moʿawia ben Abi Sofian" (Muʿāwiya b. Abī Sufyān also wrote).
- **No date** is given in the Najran pact section (spot-checked).
- **The witness list cannot fit any single date.** It includes Saʿd b. Muʿādh, Abū Hurayra and Jaʿfar b. Abī Ṭālib [SA1], with Muʿāwiya as writer. Saʿd b. Muʿādh died "shortly after the Battle of the Trench" (c. 627 / 5 AH). Abū Hurayra embraced Islam "around 7 AH (c. 629 CE)". Muʿāwiya converted at the capture of Mecca in 630 (though "some accounts" say secretly from 628). Jaʿfar was in Abyssinia for about twelve years and died at Muʿta in September 629. (Wikipedia, opened, used for standard dates.) This chronological argument is **my inference**. SA1 reports that Morrow (2013, c. pp. 109–116) discusses and tries to explain these witness objections.

**Where the two versions conflict:**

| Issue | Early Muslim sources | Long Christian text |
|---|---|---|
| Tax | Collective tribute in robes | Per-head dirham tax |
| War obligations | Najran *lends* arms and animals | No Christian compelled to equip Muslims |
| Riba | A central clause, and ʿUmar's stated reason for the expulsion | Usury appears only as a vice of the Jews |
| Witnesses and scribe | One set of names | A different set |
| Document in use | Abu Yusuf's text is the copy "in their hands" | Surfaced only in 878–79, by its own account |

**Verdicts on the long version:**
- **Addai Scher (1919),** p. 602 n. 1 (spot-checked): "Ce pacte est certainement apocryphe. Il a été forgé par les chrétiens dans le but d'engager les musulmans à les épargner." (This pact is certainly apocryphal. It was forged by the Christians in order to induce the Muslims to spare them.) He adds that "Chaque nationalité chrétienne de l'Orient en conserve une copie plus ou moins différente" (each Christian nation of the East keeps a more or less different copy) [SA1]. On ʿUmar's related edict for Ishoʿyahb, p. 623 n. 1: "probablement aussi apocryphe" (probably also apocryphal) [SA1].
- **Muhammad Hamidullah,** *Majmūʿat al-wathāʾiq al-siyāsiyya* (6th ed. 1407 AH), docs. 96–97, printed p. 180: "ولا يوجد أدنى شبهة في أن هذين النصين من الموضوعات" ("there is not the slightest doubt that these two texts are fabrications"). (Spot-checked: shamela.ws/book/23667/167.) His general criterion (p. 31) [SA1]: documents granting rights without duties, or mentioning things that did not exist in the Prophet's time, are fabrications, "كبعض العهود التي زعموا أنّ النبي … كتبها للنصارى واليهود والمجوس" (like some of the covenants they claim the Prophet wrote for the Christians, Jews and Magians). Note: Hamidullah *accepts* the short treaty (his doc. 94).
- **Lucas Van Rompay,** *Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage*, "Nagran": the pact "gave rise to the creation of apocryphal documents, such as those preserved in the 'Chronicle of Siirt'" [SA1].
- **Philip Wood,** "The Treaty between Muḥammad and the Christians of Najrān in the *Chronicle of Seert*: Negotiating the Rights of the Conquered and the Re-writing of the Past," *Al-Masāq* 33:2 (2021) 156–168, doi 10.1080/09503110.2021.1910782 (PR; abstract opened). The Chronicle "preserves an ʿAbbāsid-era re-imagination of the correspondence". The texts were "responses to Muslim efforts to produce consistent rules for non-Muslims in the caliphate". They "draw on knowledge of the Qurʾān and the Sīra to assert better rights for Christians."
- **Brandie Ratliff,** *Sinaiticus* (Saint Catherine Foundation bulletin, 2008) [SA2; not peer-reviewed, written by a specialist]. The Najran letter "seems to have been produced by viziers of the Abbasid period, who were of Nestorian origin" (citing Massignon).
- **Morrow (2013), c. pp. 109–116** [SA1; unofficial scan; verify]:
  - He concedes that the "strongest argument" against the Najran covenant is that it "appears to be completely absent in the classical Islamic sources", which is why "most scholars dismiss it as dubious".
  - He replies there is "no logical reason" for Christians to forge it, and that Scher gave no proof.

### 3.2 The Sinai "Achtiname" and Sionita's 1630 "Testamentum": editions and pre-2013 studies [SA2; key items spot-checked]

**Two different documents.**
- **The Sinai patent** is dated 3 Muḥarram AH 2, written by ʿAlī in the Prophet's mosque at Medina, with about 22 witnesses.
- **Sionita's *Testamentum*** is dated the last day of the fourth month of AH 4, at Medina, with "Moauia ben-abi-Sofian" as secretary.
- Wikipedia also says Sionita's text "is a different covenant … not a copy of the Ashtiname" (lead only).

**PRIM: Gabriel Sionita, *Testamentum et pactiones initae inter Mohamedem et Christianae fidei cultores*** (Paris: Antoine Vitré, 1630; Latin and Arabic) [SA2, archive.org BIU Santé scan].
- **Where the copy came from:** "Religiosus Pater Pacificus Scaliger ex Capucinorum familia" obtained it by "felici … casu" (lucky chance) while working in "illis regionibus" (those regions). François Hotman had Sionita translate it.
- **No mention of Carmelites or Mount Carmel** in the 1630 print.
- **Origin of the Mount Carmel story:** Paul Rycaut, *Present State of the Ottoman Empire* (1668), II.2: "the original of which was found in the Monastery of Fryars on Mount Carmel". Bespier, quoted in Bayle, objected that it was "signed at Medina … and therefore could not be made in the monastery of mount [Carmel]".
- **Witnesses** include "Moauia ben-abi-Sofian", "Abu-horain" and "Hamza ben-abdi-lmottaleb".
- **A Nissel reprint, Leiden (Elzevier) 1655,** is shown in library catalogues **[snippet only; the Princeton catalogue page was bot-blocked]**.

**Early-modern verdicts on the 1630 text** (each from an opened text):
- **Humphrey Prideaux,** *The True Nature of Imposture* (1698), pp. 160–162 (spot-checked):
  - "Grotius rejects it as a Forgery; and good reason he had".
  - "there is another particular in it, which manifestly discovers the Forgery: It makes Moawias, the Son of Abu Sophian, to be the Secretary … whereas it is certain, that Moawias, with his Father Abu Sophian, was then in Arms against him; and it was not till the taking of Mecca … that they came in unto him".
  - Muhammad, just defeated at Uhud, was in no position to be asked for protection.
- **Bayle,** *Dictionary* (English ed. 1734–38), "Mahomet", remarks AA–BB [SA2]:
  - Grotius: "forged, no doubt, by the Christians".
  - Salmasius: "I have no doubt of it's being genuine".
  - Hinckelmann and Rycaut accept it.
  - Hottinger "durst not determine".
- **Gibbon,** *Decline and Fall*, ch. 50 n. 148 [SA2]: the patent "was admitted and reprobated by the opposite taste of Salmasius and Grotius… Renaudot urges the consent of the Mohametans… but Mosheim … inclines to believe it spurious."
- **Mosheim** (tr. Maclaine) [SA2]:
  - On the Sinai edict: "undoubtedly, an artful piece of forgery" (citing Cantemir).
  - On the Sionita text: "whether it be genuine or spurious, it is certain that its contents were true".
- **Hamza's death:** Hamza b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib "was killed in the Battle of Uhud on Saturday, 23 March 625 (7 Shawwal 3 AH)" (Wikipedia, opened). He therefore could not have witnessed a document dated AH 4, as Sionita's list has him doing. This is **my inference from the opened dates**.

**PRIM/travel: Richard Pococke, *A Description of the East*, vol. 1 (London 1743), ch. XIV, p. 268ff.** [SA2]
- "The Patent of Mahomet, which he granted to the Monks of Mount Sinai; and to Christians in general".
- 18 articles and 16 witnesses. Dated "in the second year of the Hegira, the third day of the month Machorem". Translated "from the modern Greek".
- He repeats the tradition that Sultan Selim "took it from them, and granted them one under his own hand in the same terms".
- **He gives no verdict on authenticity.** (Morrow's 2022 blog lists "Pococke (1743): authentic"; SA2 found no such statement in the text.)

**J. L. Burckhardt, *Travels in Syria and the Holy Land* (1822), pp. 546–47** (spot-checked, Project Gutenberg):
- He records the monks' story: ʿAlī wrote it, and "Mohammed, who could not write" impressed his inked hand. Selim I took the original and left "a copy of the original certified with his own cipher".
- His verdict: "I do not believe it to be an authentic document. None of the historians of Mohammed … mention his having been at Mount Sinai … and it is totally contrary to history that he should have granted to any Christians such privileges".
- The document says it was written "in the mosque of the Prophet at Medina, in the second year of the Hedjra", addressed "to all the Christians". It states it was "copied from a fourth successive copy".
- His conclusion: "the former [the monks' story] is a fable and the latter a forgery."

**Georg Ebers, *Durch Gosen zum Sinai* (1872), pp. 287–88** [SA2]:
- Agrees with Burckhardt that the copy is a "betrügerisches Machwerk" (fraudulent concoction).
- Tischendorf heard the monk Kyrillos doubt the story.

**PRIM/ADV: Anton F. Haddad, *The Oath of the Prophet Mohammed to the Followers of the Nazarene*** (New York, 1902; 7 pp.; a Bahá'í publication) [SA2]:
- An English translation that accepts the text as "his valid oath and covenant".
- Its stated chain: from a Turkish copy rendered into Arabic by Naufal Effendi Naufal.
- Its 22 witnesses include "Abou Harirat" (Abū Hurayra). Abū Hurayra became Muslim around 7 AH (Wikipedia, opened), and so could not have witnessed an AH 2 document. **This is my inference.**

**PR: Bernhard Moritz, *Beiträge zur Geschichte des Sinaiklosters im Mittelalter nach arabischen Quellen*** (Abh. Preuss. Akad. Wiss., phil.-hist. Kl., 1918 Nr. 4). Section "Der angebliche Schutzbrief des Propheten"; three copies edited and translated. Spot-checked in the archive.org OCR.
- "Datierung, Stil und Inhalt beweisen jedes für sich allein schon [die Unechtheit]" (date, style and content each prove, on its own, that it is not genuine).
- **Date.** Muhammad probably came into contact with Christians and Jews in the north only around the Tabūk expedition, AH 9. Moritz also observes that "unter den 47 Schreiben des Propheten, die sein Biograph Ibn Saʿd anführt, ein solches an die Sinaiten nicht vorkommt" (among the 47 letters of the Prophet that his biographer Ibn Saʿd cites, none is addressed to the Sinai monks).
- **Form.** The text lacks the standard opening formula, and the speaking person shifts.
- **Style.** "ganz unmuhammedanisch, bisweilen kaum noch arabisch zu nennen" (entirely un-Muhammadan, at times hardly Arabic at all).
- **Anachronisms** [SA2]:
  - the Egyptian measures *ardabb* and *qadaḥ*;
  - "Sultan" as a title, not before c. 900 CE.
- **Likely date and motive.** Probably composed about 900–1000, to protect the monks against persecution under al-Ḥākim (r. 996–1020). "Daß die Fälschung besonders geschickt ausgeführt sei, läßt sich nicht behaupten" (one cannot claim that the forgery was particularly skilfully done).
- **What he concedes from the archive** [SA2]:
  - The Fatimid caliph al-Ḥāfiẓ's decree orders "die alten Verträge" (the old treaties) to be respected.
  - A Mamluk decree of Qanṣūh al-Ghūrī mentions "Schutzschreiben des Propheten und Urkunden der Chalifen" (letters of protection of the Prophet and documents of the caliphs).
  - A transfer of the original to Constantinople by Selim or Suleiman is "möglich, sogar wahrscheinlich" (possible, even probable).
  - The monastery's tradition that Selim *visited* Sinai is "sicher erfunden" (certainly invented).
  - Surviving copies are dated AH 957 and AH 968.
- **Bottom line:** the claimed Prophetic letter was being invoked by the Mamluk period at the latest, and perhaps earlier. Moritz still judges the text a Fatimid-era forgery.

**Aziz S. Atiya, *The Monastery of St Catherine in Mount Sinai* (Cairo 1950; popular booklet)** [SA2]: **undecided.**
- "it is said that the Prophet Mohammad granted the monks … a covenant".
- "the Covenant of the Prophet, whether authentic or forged, was in some way or other renewed".
- The archive holds more than 2,000 decrees (firmans); the oldest is dated 524/1130.
- **Not opened:**
  - *The Arabic Manuscripts of Mount Sinai* (1955; lending-only). Morrow's blog labels it "authentic", which is unverified.
  - The *PAPS* article (1952).

**Sinai archive inventories:**
- **Heidemann, Müller & Rāġib, *Annales islamologiques* 31 (1997)** [SA2] count 81 decrees in the archive: 6 Fatimid (524/1130–553/1158), 3 Ayyubid and 72 Mamluk.
- The Mamluk decrees were published by **Hans Ernst, *Die mamlukischen Sultansurkunden des Sinai-Klosters* (1960)**. This work was **not opened**: the Halle repository has a bot wall.
- **S. M. Stern's** BSOAS studies (1960, 1966) and *Fāṭimid Decrees* (1964): abstract only, which is silent on the Prophet's covenant.

**Heinz Skrobucha, *Sinai* (OUP 1966): NOT OPENED** (lending-only). Morrow's 2019 lecture lists him among those who held the Ashtiname "apocryphal" (ADV, secondhand).

**Historians of St Catherine's:**
- **Jean-Michel Mouton** (interview, *Clio*, 2002) [SA2]:
  - The visit story is a "légende" developed "à partir du IXe siècle" (from the 9th century onward).
  - It was one of the strategies "visant à légitimer l'existence du monastère au regard de la loi musulmane" (aimed at legitimising the monastery's existence under Muslim law).
  - His 1998 chapter was not available.
- **Brandie Ratliff** (*Sinaiticus* 2008, n. 9) [SA2]: "Since the nineteenth century, scholars have questioned aspects of the document, particularly the list of witnesses; see Mouton, 177."

**The monastery's own website (sinaimonastery.com, opened): the traditional view.**
- "According to the tradition preserved at Sinai, Mohammed both knew and visited the monastery".
- A delegation requested protection "In the second year of the Hegira, corresponding to AD 623". He "placed his hand upon the document".
- In 1517 Selim I "took the original letter of protection for safekeeping to the royal treasury in Constantinople", and left certified copies "each depicting the hand print".
- The site does not mention any authenticity debate.

**T. W. Arnold, *The Preaching of Islam* (1896/1913), and A. S. Tritton, *The Caliphs and Their Non-Muslim Subjects* (1930)** [SA2]:
- Neither discusses the Sinai or Sionita covenants in full-text searches.
- Arnold on conquest treaties: "whether verbally authentic or not, they are significant".
- Tritton lists the Prophet's Najran terms "whether historical or legendary" (p. 203). He mentions a commission from ʿAlī preserved at the monastery of Gubba the Outer and used at Baghdad (p. 83).

### 3.6 Which view is the minority?

**On the long covenants.**
- Before 2013, every specialist verdict I could open on the long Sinai and Najran/Seert texts was "forgery" or "apocryphal": Burckhardt, Ebers, Moritz, Scher and Hamidullah. Mouton calls the visit story legend.
- Atiya (1950) is undecided.
- Positive verdicts are early-modern (Salmasius, Rycaut, Hinckelmann, Renaudot) or advocacy (Haddad 1902).
- El-Wakil's 2016 abstract itself calls the forgery view "the accepted understanding".
- **The authenticity position is therefore a minority view in the specialist literature.** It has a small, active, mostly peer-reviewed group defending it since 2016.

**On the short Najran treaty.** The short tribute treaty in the Muslim sources is widely treated as reflecting a real agreement. Even Hamidullah, who rejects the Seert texts, includes it (his doc. 94), and Tritton lists its terms "whether historical or legendary". That treaty is **not** the long protective covenant that Morrow champions.

## 5. Where independent scholars directly contradict specific claims of Morrow's

Each item below gives Morrow's claim, the contradicting evidence, and where it comes from.

1. **Claim: most 19th- and 20th-century scholars held the Sinai covenant authentic.** Morrow's 2019 lecture says "Most scholars, however, believed that the Ashtiname was authentic" (20th century), and "the vast majority" in the 19th.
   - **Contradicted by El-Wakil**, Morrow's own ally: the JIS 2016 abstract describes the "accepted understanding that these are forgeries".
   - **Contradicted by Morrow's own list** of 20th-century doubters (Moritz, Skrobucha, Hamidullah, Hitti, Beeston, Mouton, the *Encyclopaedia of Islam*).
   - **Contradicted by the opened specialist verdicts:** Burckhardt 1822, Ebers 1872, Moritz 1918, Scher 1919 (Najran) and Hamidullah.
   - SA1 also reports that Morrow's own 2013 book (c. p. 109–116, verify) concedes that "most scholars dismiss" the Najran covenant "as dubious".

2. **Claim: the Sinai patent dates from 2 AH (623) and was written by ʿAlī.**
   - **Moritz (1918):** the date is impossible. Muhammad had contact with northern Christians only around 9 AH (Tabūk), and the style is "ganz unmuhammedanisch".
   - **Burckhardt:** no biography places Muhammad at Sinai, and the document's own text (Medina, AH 2, addressed to all Christians, copied from a "fourth successive copy") contradicts the monks' account.
   - **Reynolds:** the earliest copies are 16th-century.
   - **Witness list:** the Sinai list includes Abū Hurayra ("Abou Harirat" in Haddad), who became Muslim c. 7 AH. Ratliff and Mouton note that the witness list has been questioned since the 19th century. (The chronological point is my inference from standard dates.)

3. **Claim: the Prophet visited the Sinai monastery and stamped the document with his hand.** The visit claim comes from monastic tradition, which Morrow repeats (see the Castleton review and Monferrer-Sala's summary of his 2025 book).
   - **Burckhardt:** no source records the visit; "the former is a fable".
   - **Mouton:** the visit story is a "légende" developed from the 9th century as a legitimising strategy.
   - **Hussain:** the handprint image shows the back of a hand. Morrow answers that it is "art … a painting", which concedes that the image is not a physical imprint.

4. **Claim: the covenants are found in Muslim sources.** Morrow: "It is beyond dispute that the covenants of the Prophet are found in Muslim sources". Wikipedia summarises his view as the text being "replicated verbatim in numerous Islamic sources over more than a thousand years".
   - **Hussain:** "Four of them have no mention at all in Islamic sources" (secondhand).
   - **Sanni:** "no evidence of pre-sixteenth century codices, even in Islamic sources", and the Ibn Saʿd citation is "unsubstantiated" (secondhand).
   - **Moritz:** the Sinai letter is not among Ibn Saʿd's 47 Prophetic letters.
   - **Morrow 2013** (per SA1): the Najran covenant "appears to be completely absent in the classical Islamic sources".
   - **Partial support for Morrow:** Moritz himself records that Fatimid and Mamluk decrees refer to old treaties and to "Schutzschreiben des Propheten" (letters of protection of the Prophet). So *some* claimed Prophetic protection letter was being invoked before the Ottomans. That is not evidence for the 7th-century text.

5. **Claim: the long Najran covenant (from the *Chronicle of Seert*) is authentic.** The early Muslim treaty texts (Abū Yūsuf, Abū ʿUbayd, Ibn Saʿd, Balādhurī, Ibn Zanjawayh, Abū Dāwūd) give different terms:
   - a collective tribute of robes instead of per-head dirhams;
   - a duty to *lend* arms instead of an exemption;
   - riba as a core clause;
   - different witnesses.

   Specialist verdicts against it:
   - Scher (1919): "certainement apocryphe … forgé par les chrétiens".
   - Hamidullah: "لا يوجد أدنى شبهة … من الموضوعات" (not the slightest doubt … fabrications).
   - Van Rompay: "apocryphal documents".
   - Wood (2021): an "ʿAbbāsid-era re-imagination".

   The document's own story puts its appearance in 878–79 CE. Its witness list combines Saʿd b. Muʿādh (d. c. 5 AH), Abū Hurayra (Muslim c. 7 AH) and Muʿāwiya (Muslim 8 AH / 630). They could not all have witnessed it at one time; this is my inference.

6. **Claim: the 1630 "Covenant with the Christians of the World" (Sionita) is genuine.**
   - **Prideaux (1698), following Grotius:** Muʿāwiya is named as secretary in AH 4, when he "was then in Arms against him" until the conquest of Mecca.
   - **Hamza in the witness list:** he died at Uḥud in 3 AH, a year before the stated date (my inference from the opened Wikipedia date).
   - **Bespier (in Bayle):** the text says Medina, not Mount Carmel.
   - **The 1630 preface itself** says only that a Capuchin "by a lucky chance" obtained a copy. It gives no provenance for the original.
   - **Gibbon** notes that Mosheim "inclines to believe it spurious".
   - **Morrow's own 2021 article** concludes that a related "Rylands" text "appears to be an Ottoman-issued document dating from the sixteenth or seventeenth century".

7. **Claim: the Assyrian covenant is authentic.** Reynolds: the surviving copy is 17th-century, "in an Islamic Persian script that did not exist in Muhammad's day".

8. **Claim: the covenant with the Armenians of Jerusalem is authentic.** Dadoyan (Routledge 2011) titles the section "The *Alleged* Prophet's Oath to the Armenians". Her view that authenticity "cannot be established" is secondhand (R5).

9. **Method: later confirmations by Fatimid, Mamluk and Ottoman rulers prove authenticity, and continuous copies show accurate transmission.** Morrow's 2019 lecture:
   - "It has been back dated and found to be authentic";
   - "we have complete copies … from the 16th century until the 19th century".

   Against this:
   - **Monferrer-Sala:** this "conflates textual reception with historical origin". Late copies cannot "decisively illuminate seventh-century practice".
   - **Valkenberg:** the copies are "substantially younger than the originals", so "quite possible that the documents have a later origin".
   - **Atiya:** renewal happened "whether authentic or forged".

10. **Method: "the lengthy documents are the most authentic."** This reverses the textual-criticism principle that Mustafa Abu Sway cited ("shorter documents are likely more authentic"). In the Najran case, the shorter version is the one recorded in the earliest Muslim sources (§3.1).

11. **Claim: Pococke (1743) judged the Sinai covenant authentic.** This appears in Morrow's 2022 blog list. SA2 read Pococke's chapter and found **no verdict on authenticity**, only a report of the tradition.

12. **Presentation of the reviews.** The two journal reviews Morrow's sites promote most (AJISS and JSIS) were written by people Morrow thanks for help with the translations, one of whom (Sellam) signed the Covenants Initiative. This is not a contradiction of his historical claims, but it bears on "critically-acclaimed" (the Cambridge Scholars blurb).
