# V2b: Sinai / St Catherine's Monastery: verification report

**Book:** John Andrew Morrow, *The Covenants of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of the World* (2013).
**Scope:**
- Extract 01, themes J, K and L, with the matching timeline rows and inconsistencies (book pp. 8–25 [PDF 31–48]).
- Extract 03 (ch. 2): only the monastery's general history, the mosque, the Jabaliya, the eagle legend and early legends, and Napoleon. The Achtiname's documentary history is excluded (another agent covers it).

**How to read citations:**
- Book pages are given as "p. M [PDF N]". For ch. 2, PDF = printed + 25.
- Every verdict rests on a page or full text I actually opened. URLs are in the last column and in section 6.
- Archive.org scans were read through their full-text (`_djvu.txt`) files. OCR spelling is reproduced in quotations; I normalised the long s ("ſ") only.
- Wikipedia is used only for uncontroversial dates and is labelled "(Wikipedia)".

**Calendar conversions are my own.**
- Hijri dates use the tabular Islamic calendar (epoch checked: 1 Muharram 1 AH = 16 July 622 Julian). Accuracy is ±1–2 days.
- French Republican dates: 1 vendémiaire an VII = 22 Sept 1798.

**Research limits that affected this report:**
- The session's web-search quota ran out partway through. Some items could not be located and are marked Unverified, with what I tried.
- Hobbs, *Mount Sinai* (1995) is lending-restricted on archive.org, and the search-inside request returned 403.
- Britannica, Encyclopaedia Iranica, the Guardian and Church Times returned 403 or blocked automated access.

Verdict scale: Consistent / Minor difference / Inconsistent / Disputed among sources / Unverified / Opinion / Tradition (the book reports it correctly as tradition).

## 3. Discrepancies ranked by significance

1. **Thenaud mistranslated; the inferences built on it fail (p. 19 [PDF 42]).** Thenaud (1512, ed. 1884, p. 72) says the prior "se rendit mammelu quant on lui demanda compte et reliquat de son administration". He turned renegade when asked to account for his management. The book renders this as "when asked to assume its administration". On that rendering it builds three claims: Fatimid appointment of the convert prior, Muslim custody of the covenant from the 12th to the 14th century, and the Fatimids' wish that "Muslims, and not Christians, acted as the custodians". Thenaud supports none of these.

2. **Burckhardt misrepresented on the mosque's date (p. 18 [PDF 41]).** The book says Burckhardt "interprets this as proof that the mosque dates to 1381 CE". Burckhardt (pp. 543–544) uses the AH 783 record to *refute* the monks' 16th-century story and concludes that the mosque "appears therefore to have existed long before the time of Selim". That is the very inference the book presents as its own.

3. **Selim's "purchase for four thousand gold crowns" (p. 11 [PDF 34]).** The book prefers this version over all others and builds an argument for authenticity on the price ("would not have spent such a spectacular sum on a spurious document"). The only source is Cantemir (1734, p. 168, n. 50), who frames the story as told "fabulously" and the document as "true or forged". Six other accounts opened describe seizure, demand or retention. Affagart (1533–34) reports that the monks refused Turkish offers of money.

4. **Fatimid caliph conflations.**
   - p. 16: the summit mosque is dated to "Abu 'Ali al-Mansur (r. 996-1021), the tenth of the Fatimid Caliphs". The reign dates are al-Hakim's (6th caliph); "tenth" is al-Amir (1101–1130). Both bore the name Abū ʿAlī Manṣūr.
   - Ch. 2 p. 75 calls "al-Afdal" a "Fatimid Caliph" and al-Hakim's "successor". He was a vizier (1094–1121) under al-Musta'li and al-Amir.
   - p. 17 gives al-Amir as 1102–1131, against 1101–1130 (and p. 18).

5. **"All versions have an eagle" (ch. 2 p. 87).** This contradicts Cantemir's cloud, which the book itself reports on p. 11, and the omen-less accounts of Monconys, Affagart, Thenaud and Burckhardt.

6. **Wrong traveller dates.** Arundale's visit was 1833, not 1831 (p. 11; ch. 2 p. 74). Stanley's was 1852–53, not 1858 (p. 16).

7. **Bell tower dated to the 17th century (p. 18, via Arabic News).** It was built in 1871 (Mouton 2002; Art Newspaper; Sacred Destinations).

8. **"Only in the 15th century" was Jebel Musa identified as Sinai (p. 22).** Christian identification of the summit dates to the 4th century (Mouton), and Justinian built a basilica on the peak in the 6th century (monastery site; Procopius).

9. **Monastery "temporarily abandoned" by 1479 because of the fall of Byzantium (p. 23).** UNESCO says the monastery has been in use without interruption since the 6th century. Bauden (2025) documents a 1482 retreat of most monks to the al-Tur metochion after Bedouin (Awlād ʿAlī) attacks.

10. **Napoleonic expedition dated "1797-1804" (p. 24).** It ran 1798–1801 (Wikipedia; the book's own ch. 2 p. 70 says the same).

11. **Napoleon's decree date and attribution.** The book gives two dates, 8 and 19 Dec 1798. The *Correspondance* fixes the order at 29 frimaire an VII = 19 Dec 1798. The decree's articles are correctly summarised but attributed to Fazakerley "378". Fazakerley (p. 376) mentions only a Bonaparte document "promising friendship and protection". The order does not mention Muhammad; it refers to "différents titres" (various title-deeds).

12. **Relics of St Catherine "three hundred years" on the mountain (p. 22).** Historical estimates are c. 800 or the 9th century (Art Newspaper; Mouton). Burckhardt's monks said "a few years" after the monastery was built.

13. **al-Tur replacing Clysma in 1058 (p. 23).** No source found. Archaeology places the medieval port at Rāya until the 13th century, with al-Tur from the 14th century.

14. **Ottoman fort-building at five sites in 1517 (p. 23).** The al-Arish and Nakhl forts are listed as Mamluk (1516, Qansuh al-Ghawri) and Nuweiba as 1893 (Wikipedia list).

15. **Citation and quotation problems.**
    - "Hobbs 634-644" is 'Umar's reign, not pages.
    - p. 8 prints a Stanley quotation with "Jebel Munayja at the head of Wadi ad-Dayr" for Stanley's "Mount Menejia", cited "(344)" after Hobbs.
    - The same Stanley passage is cited as "120" (p. 16) and "344-345" (ch. 2). Both pages are 53–55 in the 1868 edition.
    - Monconys is cited as 228–229; the passage is on pp. 118–119 of the 1665 Lyon edition.

16. **Burckhardt's "six or seven thousand monks" (p. 9) presented as his own finding.** It is what "the prior told me".

17. **Arundale's "followers of Ali" remark applied to the Sinai Bedouin (ch. 2 p. 74).** In context it is a general remark on Arabs, made on the Cairo–Suez stage.

18. **Smaller items:**
    - The monastery mosque is closed (1986) and restored (1986) on the same page, and a 2016 report describes Muslims praying in it.
    - The Night Journey route differs between p. 21 and p. 27.
    - "nearly 500 miles" should be ≈491 km / 305 miles.
    - "1869 construction" of the Suez Canal (1869 is the opening year).
    - "Fatimids (r. 901–1171)" should begin 909.

## 4. Neutral assessment of the "Muhammad in Sinai" thesis

**What the book argues (pp. 8–16, 21; ch. 2 pp. 70, 76, 87–88).**
- There are "twenty-five years of silence" in the sira (ages 15–25 and 25–40).
- The monks' tradition fills that gap: Muhammad was a camel-driver or caravaneer for, or a guest of, the monastery. An eagle or cloud revealed his destiny, and he gave a palm-print promise that later became the achtiname.
- When news of him "disappears in the Hijaz, news of him reappears in the Sinai".

**What the sources examined show.**

1. *Islamic historical sources.*
   - Burckhardt (1822, p. 546) states that "None of the historians of Mohammed … mention his having been at Mount Sinai, neither in his earlier youth, nor after he set up as a prophet".
   - The book concedes the same in ch. 2 p. 87: the story "does not exist in any of the surviving Arabic and Islamic sources".
   - The one Islamic text found that places Muhammad at Mount Sinai is a Night-Journey narration (Sunan al-Nasa'i 450, graded *hasan* by Darussalam). It describes a miraculous stop, not a caravan visit.
   - I could not open the sira texts themselves (Ibn Ishaq/Guillaume OCR unusable; Ibn Sa'd and Tabari not available). The book's own summary of them names journeys to Syria (Busra/Bahira; the trading journey for Khadija), not to Sinai.

2. *Christian / monastic tradition.*
   - Thenaud (1512) mentions only Muhammad's hand-signed privileges kept in the mosque. He gives no visit story.
   - Affagart (1533–34) is the earliest traveller opened who reports the monks saying "qu'il avoyt esté en ce lieu en son vivant".
   - Later accounts add the camel-driver, eagle or cloud, and palm-print motifs: Monconys 1647 (via the archbishop), Thévenot (unverified), Cantemir (early 18th c., from a "fabulous treatise"), Henniker, Burckhardt ("a favourite belief of the monks") and Stanley.
   - The versions disagree on the omen (eagle, cloud, none), the material (paper, parchment, gazelle skin), Muhammad's role (friend of a monk, camel-driver, years-long servant) and the timing.

3. *Modern historians opened.*
   - Mouton (2002) treats the visit as a legend, elaborated "à partir du IXe siècle" as one of the monks' strategies to legitimise the monastery under Islamic law.
   - Stanley (1856/1868) judged that "No one can now prove or disprove the tradition", while thinking Qur'anic allusions bring it "within the range of possibility".
   - Hobbs (1995), as the book quotes him, says "there are questions about whether contact took place at all". I could not open Hobbs to confirm this.

4. *The argument from silence.* The "missing" years are a gap in the sources, not positive evidence of a stay in Sinai. The earliest dated monastic attestations of a visit found here are 16th-century, about nine centuries after the events. Mouton dates the narrative itself to the 9th century. The book's own placements of the visit are mutually inconsistent: ages 15–25, age 25, and the decade after his marriage.

**Bottom line (neutral).** The thesis rests on Christian monastic tradition recorded by European travellers from the early 16th century, and on an inference from gaps in the sira. No Islamic historical source examined records a Sinai visit (apart from the miraculous Night-Journey narration). The scholars consulted (Burckhardt, Mouton) regard the visit as a legend; Stanley regards it as unprovable. The book's claims that the monks' account "fits" and that "the evidence points to" their truthfulness are the author's opinion. The sources examined do not establish them.
