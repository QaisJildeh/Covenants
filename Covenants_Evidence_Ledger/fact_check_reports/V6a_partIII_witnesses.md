**Book under check:** John Andrew Morrow, *The Covenants of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of the World* (Angelico Press / Sophia Perennis, 2013).
**Scope:** Ch. 13 "Examining the Authorities" (pp. 323–330), Ch. 14 "Tracking the Transmissions" (pp. 331–332), Ch. 16 "General Conclusions" (pp. 353–364), Ch. 17 "Suggestions for Future Scholarship" (pp. 365–374), Appendix I (pp. 377–381), Appendix II (pp. 382–383). Ch. 15 is excluded (another agent). Pure lifespan checks are excluded (another agent) except where a witness's conversion or death date is the point of an argument.
**Compiled:** 2026-11-10.
**Page citations:** `p. M [PDF N]` from the extraction files `extract/08_ch13_ch17.md` and `extract/09_backmatter.md`; wording checked against `book/chunks/08_ch13_ch17.txt` where it mattered.

## 0. Method and labels

- **What counts as evidence.** Every verdict rests on a page or file that was opened (WebFetch, or downloaded with curl and read; page images were viewed for the key witness lists). No search-engine snippet is used as evidence. Where a colleague's earlier download was reused, the original URL is cited and the file was re-read for this report.
- **Sub-agents.** Three research sub-agents worked under the same rules. Their findings are marked **[SA-1]** (Ch. 16 rebuttals, Geniza, Maqna, Mouton, Seert dating), **[SA-2]** (Ch. 17 leads, Regraga) and **[SA-3]** (hadith terms, Sunni/Shi'i rijal ratings). Findings without a tag are my own.
- **Colleague files cross-referenced.** `verify/V0_reception_and_literature.md` (reception, editions, V0) and `verify/parts/A_assyrian_sources.md` (Assyrian covenant sources, "Part A"). Where I rely on them I say so.
- **Main reference for Companions' dates:** *TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi* (Türkiye Diyanet Vakfı, peer-reviewed scholarly encyclopedia; each article is signed and cites al-Waqidi, Ibn Sa'd, Ibn Hajar, etc.). Classical cross-checks: Ibn Hisham's *Sira* (OpenITI text). Wikipedia is used only as a labelled second source for uncontroversial points.
- **Calendar.** Hijri dates were converted with the standard tabular (civil) Islamic calendar to Julian dates (script in `v6a_me/scripts/cal.py`; ±1–2 days). Key results: 3 Muharram 2 AH ≈ 7 July 623; Rabi' II 4 AH ≈ 10 Sept – 8 Oct 625; 4 AH = 13 June 625 – 1 June 626; Badr (17 Ramadan 2 AH) ≈ 13 Mar 624; Uhud (7 Shawwal 3 AH) ≈ 23 Mar 625; 9 AH = 20 Apr 630 – 8 Apr 631; 265 AH = 3 Sept 878 – 22 Aug 879; 957 AH begins 20 Jan 1550; 968 AH = 22 Sept 1560 – 10 Sept 1561.

**Verdict labels:** Consistent / Minor difference / Inconsistent / Disputed among sources / Unverified / Opinion.

**What the table shows:**
- **No single date fits any list.**
  - The **Sinai** list (AH 2) contains at least five impossible witnesses: Abu Hurayra, Abu al-Darda', al-'Abbas, al-Fadl and 'Abd Allah b. 'Amr. A sixth, Hashim b. 'Utba, applies if Morrow's identification is accepted.
  - The **World 1630** list (AH 4) contains at least nine: Mu'awiya as scribe, Hamza, Ja'far, 'Uthman b. Maz'un, Abu al-'Aliya, both Ibn 'Abbas entries, Fudayl and 'Abd Allah b. 'Amr. Abu Hurayra is a tenth if أبو هرين is read that way.
  - The **Najran** list (AH 9–10) contains at least five: Sa'd b. Mu'adh, Ja'far, 'Uthman/'Umar b. Maz'un, Mus'ab and Abu al-'Aliya.
  - The **Assyrian** list fails at AH 4 (Mu'awiya, Hamza, Ja'far, Abu al-'Aliya and others) and again at AH 8–10 (Hamza, Sa'd b. Mu'adh, 'Uthman b. Maz'un, Abu al-'Aliya).
- **Some witnesses never fit any date in the Prophet's life.** Abu al-'Aliya converted after the Prophet's death. Mus'ab b. al-Zubayr (as printed) was born after it.
- **Morrow's own fixes do not resolve this.** He proposes yearly re-issue with dead witnesses kept "out of respect", or a single dictation in the Year of Delegations (p. 327). Neither explains a post-prophetic convert, a scribe who was not yet a Muslim on the document's own date, or the fact that the dated texts give AH 2 and AH 4 rather than AH 9.
- **Classical Muslim scholars used this exact test.** They exposed the Khaybar exemption letter because it carried the witness of Sa'd b. Mu'adh, who had died at the Trench, and of Mu'awiya, who was not yet a Muslim at Khaybar (Ibn Qayyim; Yaqut, citing al-Khatib) (1F-12). **Both names also stand on the Seert Najran list.**
- **Morrow concedes the conclusion.** He writes that "many of them were not Muslims at the time, some were dead" (p. 353) and that the lists were "appended … centuries after the fact" (p. 353).

## 3. Discrepancies ranked by significance

Ranked by how much each one bears on the book's central claim that the covenants are authentic.

1. **No witness list fits any date in the Prophet's life (pp. 323–328, 353).** At the Sinai date (AH 2), Abu Hurayra, Abu al-Darda', al-'Abbas, al-Fadl and 'Abd Allah b. 'Amr were not yet Muslims in Medina. At the World date (AH 4), the named *scribe* Mu'awiya was a pagan in Mecca, and Hamza, 'Uthman b. Maz'un and Ja'far were dead or abroad. Abu al-'Aliya converted after the Prophet's death. At the Najran date (AH 9–10), Sa'd b. Mu'adh, Ja'far, 'Uthman b. Maz'un and Mus'ab were dead or unborn. The book's Ch. 13 nonetheless calls the Sinai chain "relatively sound" and treats Sa'd b. Mu'adh as the problem; at AH 2 he is one of the few unproblematic names. Classical Muslim critics (al-Khatib, Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Qayyim) rejected the Khaybar exemption letter on this same test, citing the same names, Sa'd b. Mu'adh and Mu'awiya. *Evidence:* §2; TDV; SEERT; SIONITA; 1F-12.
2. **The hadith-science terms are misapplied (pp. 328–330).** The book makes five errors:
   - It says the covenants are "mutawatir by words and meaning but not by chain". Tawatur is defined by the chain at every level, and lafzi/ma'nawi are subtypes of it.
   - It calls them *musnad* and *muttasil* without any chain.
   - It glosses *ahad* as "a single witness", which is incorrect.
   - It says letters to rulers are exempt from isnad, which Sahih Muslim contradicts.
   - It gives the conflict rule in reverse: the majority prefer the prohibitive.

   *Evidence:* Ibn al-Salah, al-Suyuti, Ibn Hajar, al-Shahid al-Thani, al-Amidi, al-Zarkashi, Ibn Qudama, Sahih Muslim [SA-3; two spot-checked].
3. **The claimed continuity with Ibn Sa'd fails (pp. 354–355).** Ibn Sa'd has no Sinai treaty (MORITZ: not among his 47 letters; [SA-1] full-text search). His Najran treaty differs from the Seert text on the core fiscal and military clauses. Robes are paid collectively instead of a per-head tax. The Muslims receive an arms *loan* instead of the Christians being *exempted* from equipping them. The riba clause is absent from Seert. These are not "literary adornment".
4. **"The only prophetic letter of protection which was forged … was produced … by Jews" (p. 359).** This is contradicted in two ways:
   - The Khaybar exemption letter was exposed as forged by Muslim scholars from the 11th century on.
   - Specialists have judged Christian-held covenants forged: Scher, Hamidullah, Moritz, de Slane, Mouton.

   "Never admitted as authentic" is also false. Ibn Taymiyya and Gil report that such documents were accepted for a time, and Hirschfeld, who first edited the Geniza letter, defended it.
5. **'Umar and 'Ali are said to be absent from the 1630 covenant (pp. 324–325).** Both are printed in Sionita's 1630 Arabic and Latin lists. The error supports an inference of "anti-Shi'ite Sunni tampering", which therefore rests on a false premise. The book's own Appendix I shows the absence belongs to the 1655 column.
6. **The book's own textual evidence undercuts Model 1, independent issuance (pp. 327, 331–332).** World 1630, the Assyrian covenant and Seert Najran share a witness list and order. The World and Assyrian texts also share the scribe and an identical colophon (AH 4, Monday, end of the fourth month, Medina). This points to a common written ancestor.
7. **Appendix I mis-transcribes the editions (pp. 377–381):**
   - Najran: Hashim b. 'Utba, 'Abd Allah b. Hafaf and Mus'ab are omitted, and Khawwat b. Jubayr is truncated.
   - "Mus'ab ibn al-Zubayr" is placed in the Malech column; it belongs to Seert.
   - Sionita 1630: one name is split into two, two names are dropped, and "Sa'd ibn Mu'adh (twice)" is wrong.
   - The Pococke/Skrobucha forms are mapped to the wrong witnesses (the two Sa'ds swapped; Zubayr given as Sa'd b. 'Ubada; Sa'd b. 'Ubada given as Zayd b. Thabit).
   - There is no S/SH legend, and some ratings are clear errors: 'Ali "No (SH)".
8. **The Chapter 17 research leads are mis-cited (pp. 366–367).**
   - **The 1822 *Journal asiatique* pact.** The interlinear translation is in **Turkish**, not Greek. The donor is the French consul at Baghdad, **Rousseau**, not Zohrab.
   - **The "Traité de Mahomet … found in Baghdad".** This is an **1882** review mentioning a geometry treatise by "Machometus Bagdedinus", which David Gregory printed in his 1703 Euclid. It is not an 1822 covenant report.
   - **Guibert's Deir-Saferan entry.** It recycles a gazetteer line dating back at least to 1823, and it gives the wrong direction and distance.
   - **Nallino in 1899.** He presented a text sent by the Carmelite superior of Baghdad. The *Actes* call it "apocryphe certainement" (certainly apocryphal).
   - **BnF Arabe 214.** The manuscript was bought in the East by Vansleb and carries a reader's mark dated 1673. It therefore cannot be the copy Scaliger brought to France before 1630. Its catalogue record mentions no Sionita, no "Michel" and no second hand.
   - **Arpee's Persian covenant.** Its provenance is stated: New Julfa, a 17th-century copy given by the Shah. Arpee describes it as a copy of the **Sinai** charter "by modern criticism pronounced a forgery". This undercuts the book's suggestion (p. 353) that the Persian text "predates all of the others".

   *Evidence:* 1G; page images of *JA* 1822 p. 116 and *BSM* 1882 p. 145 read by me.
9. **The witness counts are wrong (p. 370).** World is given as 37–38, but Sionita prints 36. Najran is given as "the same", but Seert has 31 plus the writer. Assyria is given as 32, but Malech has 31 in English and 33 in Persian.
10. **"Correct Classical Arabic" (p. 329) contradicts both editors.** Scher calls the Seert pact's language "peu sûre et incorrecte" (uncertain and incorrect). Moritz calls the Sinai text "bisweilen kaum noch arabisch" (at times hardly Arabic at all).
11. **The Chronicle of Seert is not 9th-century by "most scholars" (p. 354).** Wood (2013) has "probably … the tenth century"; Mouton & Popescu-Belis (2005) have "vers 1036". The 878–79 date is the text's own discovery claim.
12. **Mouton's thesis is misrepresented, and the rebuttal misses it (p. 359).** Mouton and Popescu-Belis trace the text to Nestorian circles near Baghdad in the 9th century, not to invention "from scratch" by the Sinai monks. Morrow's counter-argument, that Christians were ignorant of Islam, does not engage that thesis.
13. **The Maqna paragraph misattributes positions (p. 355):**
    - Hamidullah *accepts* the Ibn Sa'd/Baladhuri Maqna letter; he rejects only the Geniza version.
    - Ibn Qayyim's verdict concerns the Khaybar letter, not Maqna.
    - Baladhuri's informant was "an Egyptian", and the text gives no place of viewing.
    - The Gibbon note concerns Ayla's Christians, not the Jews of Maqna.
    - The Qureishi rebuttal is chronologically valid only against the short Ibn Sa'd letter.
14. **Biographical errors used in the witness discussion (pp. 324–326):**
    - Sahl b. Bayda' did not die at Badr; Safwan did, and Suhayl survived.
    - 'Asim b. Thabit died at al-Raji', not Badr.
    - Abu al-'As converted *before* the conquest.
    - 'Amir b. Rabi'a "returned to **Medina** in 615": the return was to Mecca.
    - Sa'd b. 'Ubada died under 'Umar, not Abu Bakr.
    - Ja'far's death in 8 AH is described as "early years".
    - Yasir b. 'Amir, who died c. 615 in Mecca, is proposed as a witness.
    - "Abu Hudayfah ibn al-Yaman" conflates Hudhayfa b. al-Yaman with Abu Hudhayfa b. 'Utba.
15. **Several Shi'i reputations are misstated (pp. 323–324; App. I):**
    - Ibn Mas'ud is said to be held in the "highest regard … due to his loyalty to 'Ali". Al-Kashshi, al-Hilli and al-Khu'i say otherwise.
    - Thabit b. Qays is said to be "excoriated" for the Saqifa. He is in fact relied upon in al-Hilli's *Khulasa*.
    - Zayd b. Thabit, Hassan b. Thabit and Ka'b b. Malik are rated "Yes (SH)". Al-Mufid and others name them as 'Uthmanis or as hostile to 'Ali.
    - 'Abd Allah b. 'Amr's "crimes" are unsupported.
16. **The Regraga tradition is presented as history, without any citation (pp. 367–368).** The tradition is real, but specialists treat it as an Almoravid-era legend. Cornell (1998): there is "no evidence that any of these saints … predated the Barghwata polity … founded in 124/741-2". Several details have no source: the names "Hamij, Harid, Hirt", the Prophet answering in Berber, a Tifinagh Qur'an, and "the first to introduce the Qur'an". The inferences drawn from it, that the complete Qur'an existed in Mecca and that the Prophet knew all languages, are the author's own.
17. **Appendix II dates (pp. 382–383):**
    - Sinai "1561" is the 968 AH copy; the earliest dated copy is 957 AH, i.e. 1550.
    - Assyria "1845/1847" are loss dates; Part A gives 1843 as better supported.
    - "1882" is a misprint.
    - Armenia appears in the key but in no diagram.
## 4. Neutral assessment of the authenticity argument (Chapters 13–16)

### 4.1 What the book argues
Chapters 13–16 rest on six claims:
- The witness lists are on the whole sound, or at worst late additions that do not touch the content.
- The covenants are mass-transmitted (*mutawatir*) in wording and meaning and, as dictated documents, exempt from isnad criticism.
- Their content agrees with the Qur'an, the Sunna and the shari'a.
- Ibn Sa'd's short treaties are summaries of the long texts.
- Christians had neither the knowledge nor the motive to forge them, and the only known forged protection letter is Jewish.
- The several covenants were issued independently to different communities (Model 1).

### 4.2 Strongest points in the book's favour
1. **The genre is real.** Written prophetic agreements with non-Muslim communities are well attested.
   - The short Najran treaty appears in Abu Yusuf, Abu 'Ubayd, Ibn Sa'd and al-Baladhuri (V0 §3.1; TDV [necran]).
   - The Ayla and Maqna letters are in Ibn Sa'd and al-Baladhuri ([SA-1]; Hitti p. 93).
   - Michael Lecker describes the Prophet's letters to tribes and rulers as "a sizable corpus of source material", preserved by "many people, both prominent and unknown" (abstract of "The Preservation of Muḥammad's Letters", repr. 2022, doi 10.4324/9781003278894-10).
   - A Christian community holding *some* prophetic document is therefore historically plausible, and for Najran it is documented.
2. **The Sinai claim is old.** Moritz (1918), though he judged the text forged, records that the Mamluk and early Ottoman decrees refer to letters of protection "from one of the first four caliphs and the Prophet himself". The rulers named are Qaitbay, Qansuh, Khair Bek and Suleiman; Khair Bek's firman of 926 AH (1520) names "das heilige prophetische Schutzschreiben" (the holy prophetic letter of protection). A Fatimid decree of al-Hafiz orders "the old treaties" respected (V0). Moritz also thought the Ottoman removal of an "original" "möglich, sogar wahrscheinlich" (possible, even probable). The monastery thus invoked a prophetic grant before its earliest dated copy (1550).
3. **The texts are related, not independent inventions.** World 1630, the Assyrian text and Seert Najran share a witness list and order (§1B-11), and World and Assyria share a scribe and colophon. Separate denominations did not each invent a document from nothing; something was transmitted between them. This is the basis of El-Wakil's argument that a clause shared across communities is "highly unlikely" to have been forged by each (*Religions* 2019, via V0).
4. **The content draws on Islamic norms**, especially protection of churches and clergy, which the short Muslim-recorded treaties also contain.
5. **Some peer-reviewed support exists since 2013.** El-Wakil (*JIS* 2016, OUP) argues "categorical dismissal of the covenants as forgeries is no longer tenable". Zein & El-Wakil (Routledge 2022) argue the same. The question is not closed in the literature.

### 4.3 Strongest points against the book's case
1. **The witness lists fail the test classical Muslim scholarship itself uses.** Every list examined is impossible at every date in the Prophet's life (§2).
   - Al-Khatib al-Baghdadi rejected the Khaybar exemption letter, which was "in the hand of 'Ali", precisely because it carried the witness of Mu'awiya ("أسلم يوم الفتح", who became Muslim on the day of the Conquest) and Sa'd b. Mu'adh ("مات يوم الخندق في سنة خمس", who died at the Trench in year 5) (Yaqut).
   - Ibn Qayyim called it "كذب مختلق بإجماع أهل العلم" (a fabricated lie, by the consensus of the scholars), and his grounds include the same two witnesses. Ibn Taymiyya declared it forged in 701 AH (1F-12).
   - Both names stand on the Seert Najran list.
   - The book's Ch. 13 ruling that the Sinai chain is "relatively sound" overlooks five impossible witnesses (1C-21).
   - By Ch. 16 the book itself concedes the lists were "appended … centuries after the fact".
2. **The hadith-science defence misuses the discipline's terms.**
   - *Mutawatir*, *mashhur*, *musnad* and *muttasil* are properties of chains. They cannot be claimed for texts that have none.
   - Letters to rulers *were* transmitted with isnads (Sahih Muslim).
   - The conflict rule the book cites is the minority view; the majority prefers the prohibitive.
   - All four points come from Sunni and Shi'i handbooks alike ([SA-3]; 1C).
3. **The claimed continuity with Ibn Sa'd does not hold.**
   - Ibn Sa'd has no Sinai letter. Moritz: none among his 47 letters.
   - His Najran treaty and the Seert covenant differ on the central fiscal and military terms: a robe tribute against a per-head tax, and an arms *loan* to Muslims against an *exemption* from equipping them. The riba clause is missing from Seert (1F-3).
   - These are substantive differences, not "literary adornment".
4. **There is no pre-878 attestation, and specialist verdicts are negative.**
   - By its own account the long Najran text surfaced in 265/878–79 and survives in a chronicle of the 10th or 11th century.
   - Wood (2021) reads it as "an ʿAbbāsid-era re-imagination" that draws "on knowledge of the Qurʾān and the Sīra".
   - Mouton & Popescu-Belis (2005) place the origin of the Najran/Sinai text family near 9th-century Baghdad (1F-13).
   - Scher (1919), Moritz (1918), de Slane ("document apocryphe") and Hamidullah reach the same negative conclusion. Hamidullah is a Muslim scholar who accepts many prophetic documents, including the short Najran and Maqna texts.
5. **The motive argument is weak.**
   - Protective and tax-exempting documents had obvious value to the communities that held them. Ibn Taymiyya reports that forged exemption documents had long been accepted and the jizya waived ([SA-1]). Gil notes rulers "sometimes" accepted Khaybari claims.
   - The book's premise that Eastern Christians were ignorant of Islam conflicts with the Nestorian-secretary thesis (Mouton/Massignon) and with Wood's analysis.
6. **The shared textual features point to adaptation.** The common witness list, scribe and colophon (1E-6) are what one expects if one text was adapted for several communities, which is Scher's observation that "chaque nationalité chrétienne de l'Orient en conserve une copie plus ou moins différente" (each Christian nation of the East keeps a more or less different copy; PO 13 p. 602 n. 1, re-read by me). They are not what one expects from separate dictations to separate communities.
7. **Methodological standards.**
   - Wadad al-Qadi's study of early Islamic letters insists on *genre-specific* criteria for authenticity (chapter introduction, pp. 215–276, Gerlach reissue 2021).
   - Landau-Tasseron shows how compilers reshaped the accounts of the year-9 delegations (BSOAS 1986, abstract).
   - The book applies neither diplomatic analysis (formulae, script, material) nor redaction analysis. Its strongest tools are content agreement and analogy with hadith, and §1C shows the analogy is misapplied.
8. **The data are handled loosely in places.** Appendix I has mis-transcriptions (1H). The claim that 'Ali and 'Umar are absent from 1630 is false (1B-3/4) yet supports a "tampering" inference. The witness counts are wrong (1D-3).

### 4.4 Overall (my assessment, offered as such)
- **What the book shows.** The prophetic-covenant *genre* is real. A Najran agreement existed. Christian communities from the 9th century on (Najran/Seert) and in Mamluk–Ottoman Sinai held and successfully invoked texts in the Prophet's name.
- **What it does not show.** It does not establish that the extant long texts go back to the Prophet. Its central evidential moves fail when checked against the sources it invokes:
  - the witness lists;
  - the hadith-science classification;
  - the Ibn Sa'd comparison;
  - the "only forgery was Jewish" claim.
- **The best-supported reading.** The positions most compatible with the material I opened are the mainstream pre-2013 view and a cautious middle view:
  - Mainstream view: the long covenants are later compositions, 9th century or later, by their own testimony and on internal evidence.
  - Middle view: they *may* preserve memories of genuine prophetic protection agreements, as Valkenberg and the hedged Zein & El-Wakil 2020 piece suggest (V0).
- **Where the case stands.** The pro-authenticity case remains a minority position in the specialist literature. Even its defenders concede that no original survives. Morrow's own 2021 article dates a related "Rylands" text to the 16th–17th century Ottoman period (V0).
