# V4: Verification of Morrow, *The Covenants of the Prophet Muhammad with the Christians of the World* (2013)
## Chapter 5, "The Prophet Muhammad and the Christians of the World" (pp. 139–176), and the Chapter 10 texts (World I–IV; 1538, 1630 and 1655 reproductions)

**Inputs.** I read the extraction `extract/05_ch5.md` in full and the Chapter 10 parts of `extract/07_texts.md`. I checked the book's exact wording for key items against `book/chunks/05_ch5.txt`: "folios 245-246", "Hatur 1380", "1671 and in 1672", "1688", "four times during the first year", "1655, 1638, 1668, 1690, and as late as 1888", "James Thayer Addison", "Rabi' Ashar", "Plagiarizing Grassi" and "a certain Mr. Legrand". All of these are the book's own printed text, not extraction or OCR errors.

**IDs.** T# is a row of the extraction's timeline table (§2). C# is a numbered claim in its §4.

**Method.** Every verdict rests on a page I opened, cited by URL. Where a page was blocked, I say so. Wikipedia is used only as a lead or for uncontroversial dates, and is labelled "(WP)". Rows T39–T71, T78, T97, T100, T113, T114, T119 and T123, and claims C88–C111 and C174–C177 (printing history, Franco-Ottoman context, reigns), were checked by a forked sub-agent. I condensed its findings in §1h; its full file is `verify/parts/A_printing_politics.md`.

**Access limits.** Gallica, HathiTrust, the Fihrist web front-end, Marsh's Library, the DDB Hamburg record, Encyclopaedia Iranica, Britannica, T&F, MDPI and ResearchGate blocked automated access. I therefore could not view images of BnF Arabe 214 itself. Statements about its contents rest on the BnF catalogue record and on de Slane.

**Calendar method.** I computed all calendar conversions with standard arithmetic:
- Coptic year: 1 Thout AM 1 = 29 Aug 284 (Julian), with 30-day months. Check: 1 Thout 1592 AM = 11 Sep 1875 (Gregorian), as Wikipedia (WP) states.
- Islamic year: tabular calendar, civil epoch 16 Jul 622 (Julian), leap years 2, 5, 7, 10, 13, 16, 18, 21, 24, 26 and 29 of the 30-year cycle.

The script is at `scratchpad/scripts/cal.py`.

## 3. Discrepancies ranked by significance (most serious first)

1. **The identification of BnF Arabe 214 as the manuscript Scaliger brought and Sionita printed (pp. 144–146; "there is no question", p. 145).** This is the chapter's central documentary claim, and the evidence runs against it.
   - The BnF's own record gives the provenance as "Acheté par Vansleb en Orient", with Vansleb seals. Wansleben was in Egypt in 1663–64 and 1672–73.
   - The record carries a reading mark dated 1673.
   - The mark Morrow reads as Sionita's belongs to a "Ġabriyāl Ṣahyūn al-**Ziftāwī**". Zifta is in the Egyptian Delta; Sionita was from Ehden, Lebanon.
   - The 1538 text differs from the 1630 print in its opening, scribe ('Ali vs Mu'awiya), date (none vs Monday at the end of the 4th month, 4 AH) and witnesses (30 vs 36).
   - A Cambridge manuscript (UL Dd.11.33) contains "two copies of the covenant … transcribed by Antoine Vitré", the 1630 printer.
   - Morrow's rebuttal of the Vansleb provenance ("chronologically incorrect") depends on wrong travel dates (1671/1672) and on assuming the conclusion. He himself hedges on pp. 168 and 176, and his 2021 and 2025 work places the 1630 text in a separate "Mu'awiyah lineage".
   - Sources: BnF record; de Slane p. 55; SLUB images; Fihrist TEI for Dd.11.33; Crossref (Morrow 2021); Monferrer-Sala 2026.

2. **The covenant's date is mistranslated (p. 147; World III, p. 236).** The 1630 Arabic has *yawm al-ithnayn tamām arbaʿat ashhur min al-sana al-rābiʿa* ("Monday, at the completion of four months of the fourth year"). The Latin has "die Lunae ultima mensis quarti". The book's "second day of the month of Rabi' Ashar" names a non-existent month and turns "Monday" into "second day". This matters because Morrow builds dating arguments on it (the Jewish conflict; "the same year as the Assyrian covenant"). On the CE side, 625 (Twiss) is right for the stated month; Hinckelmann's 626 is not.

3. **"Dr. Addison" is misidentified, and his position reversed (pp. 153–154, 157).** The book lists James Thayer Addison (1887–1953) among those who treat the covenant as factual and credits "Dr. Addison" with a "detailed defense … summarized by Bayle". The source ("Bayle 1735: 241" = *General Dictionary* I, 1735, art. "ADDISON (LANCELOT)") is about Lancelot Addison (d. 1703), Dean of Lichfield. He treated the treaty as a temporary "artifice" and reported an Arabist's view that Scaliger probably forged it. The roster of "defenders" is inflated accordingly. Relatedly, the allegation that Scaliger forged the text (p. 142) is the informant's, as reported in the Addison article, not Bayle's own.

4. **The publication history is overstated (p. 151; p. 146).**
   - No evidence was found for "four times during the first year".
   - No 1668 or 1888 edition was found.
   - The 1638 printing is Fabricius's Latin-only text.
   - Printings in 1645, 1660, 1661 and 1683/84 are omitted.
   - The "1688" English translation is a later edition. Rycaut printed an English translation in 1666–68.

   The book uses the inflated history to argue strong demand and early European acceptance.

5. **The Coptic dates in the MS are mis-transcribed and mis-converted (pp. 145, 175).** The BnF reads 9 Hatur **1308**; Morrow prints 1380, which would be 1663. The BnF's conversions, copied by Morrow, are each one year late: 14 Baba 1336 is 1619, not 1620, and 9 Hatur 1308 is 1591, not 1592. The 1254 = 1538 conversion, and the Monday 24 June 1538 colophon, are correct.

6. **The Mount Carmel chronology (p. 139).** The book says Ricaut's claim is "chronologically … correct" because a Catholic monastery stood on Mount Carmel in Scaliger's day. A lead source (WP) says the Carmelites returned only in 1631, after Pacifique's 1627–28 stay. This needs a specialist source (Giordano 1994) for confirmation. Note also that the Medina dating already contradicted Mount Carmel in Bespier's 17th-century critique.

7. **Van Dyck (p. 154).**
   - His appendix explicitly credits Grassi (1825), so "plagiarizing" is wrong.
   - The "war of Greek independence" sentence is Grassi's (1825), not Van Dyck's own reaction in 1881.
   - There is no textual basis for "he himself believed that it was genuine".
   - His name is spelled Van Dyck.

8. **The 1795 Committee (p. 155).** Lefebvre-Villebrune was not "replaced by Mr. Legrand" on the project. Legrand replaced him as nominee for keeper of modern manuscripts at the Bibliothèque nationale. The printing order itself was formally rescinded on 3 brumaire IV (25 Oct 1795), the "prétendu Traité". The other session dates and numbers are correct.

9. **Printing-history claims used as context (pp. 148–150; fork).**
   - A French printed Qur'an c. 1536, and "the French were the first": no evidence. The first is Venice 1537–38.
   - The 1514 "Islamic book" is a Christian Horologion.
   - The Ottoman printing "ban" is disputed.
   - The 1536 capitulations with Holy Places protection: the 1536 text is contested, and the Holy Places clauses come only in 1604.
   - Chejne's dates for Aleppo and Lebanon are wrong.

10. **Classification of scholars.** Mosheim is labelled an opponent. His text calls the Testament "dubious" and finds the forgery hypothesis "not incredible", but rejects the style argument and accepts its "subject matter". Rehatsek (1877) says he "gives no opinion of his own". Layard reports both views rather than endorsing one. Twiss argues from antiquity while calling it the "soi-disant testament". Morrow's other labels match the texts opened: Salmasius, Hinckelmann, Renaudot and Sionita as defenders; Hottinger as neutral; Grotius, Voetius, Hoornbeek, Bespier, Prideaux, Bayle, Döllinger and Gieseler as opponents.

11. **Smaller points.**
   - Vansleb's travel years.
   - Pacifique's arrival in Syria (1626/27, not 1628).
   - Sionita's chair (1614, not 1618).
   - Grassi's date (1825, not 1826).
   - The folio numbers in Arabe 214.
   - The *Chronicle of Seert* (10th–11th c., not 9th).
   - Grassi's "Charte mahométane" epigraph refers to Islamic law, not the Testament.
   - Lamartine names an Austrian envoy, not "European diplomats".
   - World III lists 38 witnesses (a duplicated Zayd ibn Arqam and a split name) where the print has 36.

## 4. Scholarly assessment of authenticity (both sides, neutrally stated)

**What is not in dispute.**
- An Arabic text titled *al-ʿAhd wa-l-shurūṭ* was printed in Paris in 1630 from a copy Pacifique de Provins brought from the Levant, with a Latin version commissioned by Hotman.
- A related but distinct recension of a "charter granted to all Christians" was copied in Egypt in 1538 in a Coptic/Jacobite apologetic anthology (BnF Arabe 214).
- De Slane (1883–95) already called it "one of the numerous redactions of this apocryphal document".

**Arguments and authorities for authenticity, or at least for early origin.**
- **Renaudot (1713).** The Testament is found "in various manuscripts". Even Muslims do not doubt it. Those who call it spurious are only "moderately versed" in Arabic literature. He holds that Christian affairs were in fact ordered according to its provisions. (https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_r5tk9rAzY6AC)
- **Salmasius and Hinckelmann (per Bayle 1737).** Salmasius held it a genuine "league and covenant", linking it to Elmacin's report that Muhammad granted Christians safe-conduct for tribute. Hinckelmann held the same opinion. (https://archive.org/details/dictionaryhi_bayl_1738_04)
- **Twiss (1887).** Whatever its authenticity, the document is of "haute ancienneté". Its term "conditions" fits Islamic law. Forgers would have avoided wording inadmissible in Muslim courts. (https://archive.org/details/ledroitdesgensou01twis)
- **Manuscript evidence.** The 1538 manuscript shows that a version circulated about 90 years before 1630, so Scaliger did not invent the genre in 1628–29. Further copies exist in Cambridge (Dd.11.33) and Manchester (Rylands, per Morrow 2021). Morrow (2021) suggests the Rylands/Shidyāq covenant could be "the missing link" between the Najran covenant of the *Chronicle of Seert* and the 1630 *Testamentum*. (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fihristorg/fihrist-mss/master/collections/cambridge%20university/Dd_11_33.xml ; https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390/rel12090751)
- **Parallels with the Nestorian charter tradition.** The substance parallels the charter for Isho'yahb II and the Christians of Najran reported by Bar Hebraeus, Mari and 'Amr (Layard 1849; Rehatsek 1877). Even sceptics such as Döllinger and Gieseler accepted that Muhammad gave some Arabian Christians letters of protection. (https://archive.org/details/b29350207_0001 ; https://archive.org/details/jasb-volume-13-for-1877-issue-no-35 ; https://archive.org/details/historyofchurcht02dl ; https://archive.org/details/textbookofchurch01gies)
- **Recent defenders.**
  - Morrow (2013 and later).
  - Zein and El-Wakil, *The Covenants of the Prophet Muḥammad* (Routledge 2022/23). The reviewer Negoiță (JAAR 93:4, 2025) describes the book as "systematically debunking the longstanding skepticism", using "transmission nuances, calendar synchronization, and scribal convention analyses".
  - Sellam (AJIS 2015) reviewed Morrow 2013 favourably.
  - Considine (*Religions* 2016) uses the covenants as a framework for pluralism.

  (https://academic.oup.com/jaar/article/93/4/740/8316999 ; https://www.ajis.org/index.php/ajiss/article/view/977 ; https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390/rel7020015)

**Arguments and authorities for later composition or forgery.**
- **Internal anachronism.** Mu'awiya is named as secretary in 4 AH, but he joined Islam at the conquest of Mecca (8 AH/630), or at most at Hudaybiyya (628). Prideaux (1697, via Bayle rem. [BB]) made this point. Morrow himself concedes that the scribe and date are later "Umayyad additions" (p. 167).
- **Historical implausibility (Prideaux).** In 4 AH, soon after Uhud, Muhammad was not powerful enough for anyone to seek his protection on such terms. **Provenance contradiction (Bespier, via Bayle):** a text "signed at Medina" cannot have been made at Mount Carmel.
- **Content too favourable to Christians.** Grotius: "haud dubie a Christianis suppositum, ut sub obtentu tanti nominis Musulmanis aequioribus uterentur". Tychsen and Döllinger: it would put Christians in a better condition than Muslims; it bears "every sign of invention". Gieseler: "doubtless … spurious". Mosheim found the hypothesis of fabrication by Syrian and Arabian monks "not incredible", comparing the Sinai patent, which he thought "beyond all controversy fraudulently drawn up". (https://archive.org/details/dictionaryhi_bayl_1738_04 ; https://archive.org/details/historyofchurcht02dl ; https://archive.org/details/textbookofchurch01gies ; https://archive.org/details/institutesofeccl183901mosh)
- **Style and attestation.**
  - Lancelot Addison's Arabist found nothing Qur'anic in its style and suspected Scaliger (*General Dictionary* I, 1735). Mosheim discounted the style argument.
  - Morrow concedes (p. 139) that the text is absent from Islamic sources.
  - It survives in Christian manuscripts and in divergent redactions: two scribes, differing witness lists, dated and undated forms.
  - The 1630 publication followed a 1628 fiscal "persecution" of Levantine Christians, which Pacifique himself links to the text.
- **Catalogue and archival judgments.** De Slane (1883–95): "document apocryphe". Guillaume (1907): "document apocryphe", "prétendu Traité". The Revolutionary Committee rescinded its own printing order in October 1795.
- **The parent Najran tradition.** Philip Wood dates the *Chronicle of Seert* to the 10th–11th century and has a 2021 study whose title treats the Najran treaty as a "re-writing of the past" (*Al-Masāq* 33:2; I read only the title and metadata). This bears on Morrow's argument (pp. 146–147, 175) that the World covenant shares an ancient origin with the Najran text. (https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah30539 ; https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/09503110.2021.1910782)
- **Assessment of Morrow's method.** Monferrer-Sala's review of Morrow's 2025 sequel (*Mediterranea* 11, 2026, pp. 403–408): "the work at times conflates textual reception with historical origin … its historical argumentation is more persuasive as advocacy than as a decisive demonstration of authenticity". (https://journals.uco.es/mediterranea/article/download/18755/17486/60103)

**When and by whom, if later.** The sources opened propose these alternatives (none is proven):
- Christians generally, to win milder treatment (Grotius).
- Monks of Syria or Arabia (the Grotian view, as Mosheim reports it).
- The Carmelites in Palestine, as the source of Scaliger's copy (Döllinger's provenance).
- Scaliger himself (Addison's informant, 1678/1687). The 1538 manuscript rules this out for the genre, though not for the specific 1630 recension.
- For the Najran parent text, an Abbasid-era Church of the East milieu (Wood 2021, from its title; the 878 "discovery" is reported by the *Chronicle*, which another agent is checking).

**Neutral summary.** The documentary record supports three points:
1. Versions of a "charter of Muhammad to all Christians" circulated among Arabic-speaking Christians by 1538.
2. The 1630 recension contains at least one clear anachronism (Mu'awiya as scribe in 4 AH) and has no Islamic attestation.
3. Opinion has been divided since 1630.

On the authorities opened here, most specialist historians from Grotius to Gieseler and the BnF cataloguers judged the text apocryphal. Renaudot, Salmasius, Hinckelmann and, among moderns, Morrow and Zein & El-Wakil defend an authentic core. Monferrer-Sala's 2026 review judges Morrow's approach "more persuasive as advocacy than as a decisive demonstration of authenticity".
